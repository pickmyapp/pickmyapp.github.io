Notion vs Asana for Project Tracking

  • Project Management
  • Published Oct 11, 2026
  • Updated: October 2026
  • 10 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

Asana is better for pure project tracking: purpose-built tasks with dependencies, timelines, portfolios, and workload views. Notion is better when your projects live inside your docs: wikis, specs, and meeting notes that link straight to task databases. Pricing: Notion Plus $10 and Business $20 per user/month (annual); Asana Starter $10.99 and Advanced $24.99 (annual). The catch — the Asana features that justify picking it (portfolios, workload, goals) all need the $24.99 Advanced tier. Summary: choose Asana if tracking is the job; choose Notion if knowledge plus light tracking is enough; many teams use both. Prices checked October 2026.

Notion and Asana are the two tools most teams shortlist for organizing work, but they answer different questions. Asana asks "what needs to happen, who owns it, and when is it due?" Notion asks "where does everything we know live, and can tasks live there too?" That distinction — purpose-built tracking versus a flexible workspace with tracking bolted on — decides the whole comparison. This guide compares how each one tracks projects, what each costs at the tier you actually need, which teams fit which tool, and what migration really involves, using prices checked in October 2026. For how Asana stacks up against its closer rivals, see our Asana vs Monday vs ClickUp comparison.

The core split: tool vs workspace

Asana is a work-management platform where every object is a task, project, or portfolio. The structure is opinionated: tasks have assignees and due dates, projects have timelines, teams have portfolios, and the whole thing is designed around execution. Notion is a connected workspace where every object is a page, and pages can contain databases that act like tasks. The structure is yours to invent: a project tracker is a database view you designed, sitting next to the wiki, the specs, and the meeting notes that explain why the work exists.

Neither approach is strictly better — they optimize for different failure modes. Asana fails teams that need flexibility: strict structure feels like a straitjacket for creative or exploratory work. Notion fails teams that need consistency: flexibility means every team builds its tracker differently, and cross-team reporting becomes a negotiation about whose database schema wins. The rest of this guide is about which failure mode your team would rather manage.

How Notion handles project tracking

In Notion, project tracking is a database with views. You create a tasks database with properties for status, assignee, due date, and priority, then view it as a board, timeline, calendar, or list depending on the question you're asking. Subtasks and dependencies are supported, charts are unlimited on paid plans, and database automations can update statuses or assign tasks when conditions change. The killer feature is context: the task links directly to the spec doc, the meeting note, and the wiki page that explains it, so nobody hunts through a second tool for the "why."

The weaknesses are structural. Notion has no native workload view — you can approximate resource planning with filtered views and rollups, but it's a build-it-yourself exercise. Portfolio-level reporting across many databases requires consistent schemas, which real teams rarely maintain. And setup is a project in itself: the first week of Notion is building the system, and teams without a designated owner tend to drift into messy, half-adopted trackers. If your team wants simple kanban-style tracking without the build-out, our Trello alternatives guide covers lighter options.

How Asana handles project tracking

In Asana, project tracking is the product. Tasks are first-class citizens with assignees, due dates, dependencies managed directly from the timeline, approvals, and proofing. The Advanced plan adds portfolios (see every project at once), workload (see who's overloaded across the portfolio), goals (tie projects to company objectives), and time tracking. Rules automate routing and status changes, and the whole system works the same way for every team, which is exactly what makes cross-team reporting possible without a schema negotiation.

The weaknesses are cost and rigidity. Asana's free Personal plan (up to 10 teammates, unlimited tasks) is genuinely good for small teams, but the features that make Asana a serious tracking tool — workload, portfolios, goals, time tracking, Asana AI — all require the Advanced plan at $24.99 per user per month. A team of 10 pays $3,000 a year for what Notion Plus does at $1,200 with more docs thrown in. And the opinionated structure that makes reporting easy makes edge cases hard: if your workflow doesn't fit tasks-projects-portfolios, you're fighting the tool instead of using it.

Views, dependencies, and workload

Project views

Both offer boards, lists, timelines, and calendars. Asana's timeline is the more mature Gantt-style view, with dependencies you can draw and manage directly on the chart. Notion's timeline view covers the same basics, plus charts and the ability to embed the tracker inside a doc with prose around it. For straightforward "what's happening when" questions, they're comparable. For complex multi-project scheduling with dependency chains, Asana's is built for the job and Notion's is adapted to it.

Dependencies

Asana treats dependencies as a core concept: finish-to-start links, dependency management from the timeline, and automatic date shifting when a predecessor moves. Notion supports dependencies as database relations, but there's no native critical-path logic and shifting a predecessor doesn't cascade dates without automation. If your projects have real dependency chains — construction, launches, manufacturing — Asana is meaningfully better. If your dependencies are mostly informational, Notion's relations suffice.

Workload and resource planning

This is Asana's clearest win and Notion's clearest gap. Asana's Advanced plan includes Portfolio Workload: see how work is distributed across people, spot who's overloaded, and rebalance before burnout. Notion has no native workload view; teams approximate it with filtered database views and rollups, which takes real setup and still doesn't match the dedicated view. For managers whose job includes capacity planning, this single feature can justify the Advanced tier.

Automation

Both automate routine tracking chores. Asana's rules engine (custom rules on Starter and up) handles routing, status changes, and notifications without code; AI Studio on higher plans adds agentic workflows. Notion's database automations cover status updates, assignments, and notifications, with custom agents available on a credit model ($10 per 1,000 Notion credits). For teams that want to connect tracking to the rest of their stack — syncing tasks with email or CRM — general automation tools like n8n or Make integrate with both platforms.

Which teams fit each

Choose Notion for project tracking when

Your projects are knowledge-heavy: agencies writing specs, startups documenting decisions, content teams where the brief and the task are inseparable. Notion shines when the same page holds the plan, the tasks, and the documentation — a product team that keeps PRDs, roadmaps, and sprint boards in one workspace gets more value from that unity than from a better Gantt chart. It's also the natural pick for teams already living in Notion: adding a tasks database to an existing workspace is free and instant, while spinning up Asana is a second system to maintain.

Choose Asana for project tracking when

Execution is the job: operations teams, marketing teams running campaigns, agencies managing client delivery with dependencies and deadlines. Asana fits when multiple teams need to report in the same structure, when managers need workload and portfolio views, and when "who's doing what by when" is asked daily. The learning curve is shorter than Notion's — new hires understand tasks and timelines immediately, while Notion's flexibility requires onboarding into your specific build. For a broader tool landscape, our automation comparisons cover what pairs well with either.

The both option

Many teams use both, and it's the most common real-world answer: Asana for project execution, Notion for team wiki and documentation. The cost is maintaining two systems and the discipline to keep each in its lane. It works when the split is explicit — "tasks live in Asana, knowledge lives in Notion" — and fails when both try to do both.

Migration considerations

Moving tracking from Notion to Asana is straightforward in data and messy in structure. Export each Notion database as CSV and use Asana's CSV importer to map columns to task fields — name, assignee, due date, custom fields. Page content, linked databases, and relational context don't transfer cleanly, so keep Notion as a read-only archive for the docs and recreate only the active task structures in Asana. Budget a cleanup pass: assignees, statuses, and custom fields rarely map one-to-one, and someone has to own the reconciliation.

Moving from Asana to Notion is straightforward in data and messy in discipline. Tasks export as CSV and import into a Notion database easily, but you lose dependencies' cascading logic, workload views, and portfolio reporting with no native replacement. The migration that fails is the one that treats Notion as "Asana but cheaper" — it works when the team genuinely wants docs-plus-tracking and accepts building the system, and it fails when the team wanted Asana's structure without Asana's price. Either direction, run both tools in parallel for two to four weeks and migrate projects as they start new cycles, not mid-flight.

Our verdict

Choose Notion if

  • Your projects live inside documentation — specs, wikis, meeting notes, and tasks in one workspace.
  • You want tracking plus knowledge at $10/user/month, not $24.99.
  • Your team is small and your workflows are simple enough for databases and views.
  • Someone will own the workspace setup and keep it coherent.

Choose Asana if

  • Project tracking is the core need — dependencies, timelines, portfolios, workload.
  • Multiple teams need consistent structure and cross-team reporting.
  • Managers need capacity planning and portfolio-level visibility (Advanced tier).
  • You want new hires productive on day one with minimal onboarding.

Start with the free tier of whichever you're leaning toward — both have genuinely usable free plans — and build one real project in it before committing. If the project has dependency chains and a manager asking "who's overloaded," you'll feel Asana's pull within a week. If the project keeps spawning docs that explain the tasks, you'll feel Notion's. The right tool is the one that matches the shape of your work, not the one with the better landing page.

Frequently asked questions

Is Notion or Asana better for project tracking?

Asana is better for pure project tracking: purpose-built tasks with assignees, due dates, dependencies, timelines, portfolios, and workload views. Notion is better when projects live inside documentation — wikis, specs, and meeting notes that link directly to task databases. Many teams use both: Asana for execution, Notion for knowledge.

Is Asana really more expensive than Notion?

At the tier that matters, yes. Notion Plus is $10 per user per month and Business is $20 on annual billing. Asana Starter is $10.99 but the features that make Asana worth choosing — portfolios, workload, goals, time tracking — require the Advanced plan at $24.99 per user per month. For a 10-person team, that's $3,000 a year versus $1,200 on Notion Plus.

Can Notion replace Asana for project management?

For small teams with simple workflows, yes — Notion databases with timeline, board, and calendar views handle basic tracking, and everything sits next to your docs. For cross-functional teams with dependencies, resource planning, or portfolio reporting, Asana's purpose-built structure is hard to replicate in Notion without heavy configuration.

How do I migrate from Notion to Asana?

Export each Notion database as a CSV file, then use Asana's CSV importer to map columns to task fields (name, assignee, due date, custom fields). Page content and linked databases don't transfer cleanly, so keep Notion as a read-only archive for the docs and recreate only the active task structures in Asana. Plan for a cleanup pass — assignees and statuses rarely map one-to-one.

Do Notion and Asana have free plans?

Both do. Notion's free plan is best for individuals (unlimited pages for solo use, limited history, up to 10 guests). Asana's Personal plan is more team-friendly, supporting up to 10 teammates with unlimited tasks and projects on the free tier — the most generous free plan of the two for actual team tracking.

Conclusion

Choose Asana for project tracking when tracking is the job — dependencies, timelines, portfolios, and workload views are purpose-built and worth the $24.99 Advanced tier for teams that need them. Choose Notion when your work is knowledge plus light tracking, and you'd rather pay $10 a seat for a workspace where docs and tasks live together. And don't dismiss the both option: Asana for execution and Notion for knowledge is how a lot of real teams actually run. Verify current pricing before you buy — both vendors bill more month-to-month, and Asana's paid tiers have minimum seat counts.
