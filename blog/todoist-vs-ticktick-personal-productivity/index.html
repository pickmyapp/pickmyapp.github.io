Todoist vs TickTick for personal productivity

  • Project Management
  • Published Nov 9, 2026
  • Updated: November 2026
  • 12 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

For most people picking a personal task manager in 2026, TickTick is the better value and Todoist is the better capture tool. TickTick Premium costs $49.99 a year against $60 a year for Todoist Pro — about $10 a year less — while bundling a Pomodoro timer, habit tracker, full calendar and Eisenhower Matrix that Todoist does not offer at any price. Todoist wins where it matters most for heavy users: its natural-language input parses dates and repeats more reliably, and it has 80-plus native integrations against TickTick's sparse set. Both free plans are usable, but they hit different walls: Todoist free stops at 5 projects, TickTick free at 5 habits and 2 reminders per task. Pick TickTick if you want one app that does everything; pick Todoist if you capture 20-plus tasks a day by typing and live inside Gmail, Slack or Google Calendar.

What they cost — and the $10 gap that isn't the story

Todoist Pro costs $5 a month billed annually — $60 a year — or $7 a month billed monthly ($84 a year). TickTick Premium costs $49.99 a year on TickTick's website, about $4.17 a month, or $4.99 a month through the App Store. At list price, TickTick is roughly $10 a year cheaper. Both prices are recent: Todoist raised Pro on December 10, 2025, from $48 a year to $60, a 25% jump, and TickTick's price rose from $35.99 a year to $49.99. Most comparison articles still quote $35.99 and $48 — both are stale — though TickTick has also run $35.99 as a promotional offer, so check the checkout page before you pay: the list price is $49.99, and a discount may or may not be running when you buy.

The monthly-billing penalty is real on both sides. Todoist monthly costs $24 a year more than annual ($84 vs $60). TickTick's App Store monthly at $4.99 totals $59.88 over twelve months, about $9.89 more than the annual plan. Neither penalty is ruinous, but both are the kind of money that adds up across the five or six subscriptions a typical knowledge worker already pays for.

What does the $10 gap actually buy? On TickTick's side, the built-ins: a Pomodoro timer, a habit tracker with streaks, a full calendar with week and month views, and an Eisenhower Matrix. Todoist offers none of those natively at any price, so the honest comparison prices the replacement apps. A decent habit tracker runs $30–$50 a year and a focus timer $20–$40 — call it $50–$90 a year of extra software to match TickTick's bundle, against a $10 saving. That is the value argument for TickTick. The counter-argument is integration depth: Todoist connects natively to 80-plus apps — Gmail, Slack, Google Calendar, Outlook, GitHub, Toggl — plus 8,000 more through Zapier and a first-party public API. TickTick's native integrations are sparse, mostly Gmail, Google Calendar and Alexa. If your tasks arrive by email and chat, Todoist's integrations are worth more than $10 a year.

One more cost to name: the team tier. Todoist Business is $8 a user a month billed annually ($96 a year) or $10 monthly, with 500 team projects, roles and up to 1,000 members. TickTick has no team plan at all — no per-seat pricing, no workspace, no admin controls. A solo user who later needs a shared team workspace has no upgrade path inside TickTick, and that is a real switching cost to price in on day one.

Capture and daily use: where Todoist earns its price

The feature that decides this comparison for heavy users is the least glamorous one: typing a task and having the app understand it. Todoist's natural-language Quick Add is consistently rated the best in the category. Type "call the accountant every other Tuesday at 9am" and it parses the date, the repeat and the wording without extra taps. TickTick's natural-language input is good, and slightly less sophisticated — the kind of difference you notice on your twentieth task of the day, not your second. For a user who captures 20-plus tasks a day by typing, this is the deciding feature, and it is the main reason to pay Todoist's extra $10 a year.

Both apps handle the daily loop — capture, plan, do, review — but they organise it differently. Todoist organises by projects (5 free, 300 on Pro), filters (3 free, 150 on Pro), labels and priorities P1–P4, with sections and sub-tasks inside each project. Its views are list and board, plus a calendar layout on Pro and a two-way Google Calendar sync. TickTick organises by lists (9 free, 299 on Premium) with 99 tasks per list free and 999 on Premium, sub-tasks, tags and checklists, and its views are list, Kanban and a built-in calendar — basic on free, full week and month views on Premium. TickTick organises by lists (9 free, 299 on Premium) with 99 tasks per list free and 999 on Premium, sub-tasks, tags, priorities and checklists, and its views are list, Kanban and a built-in calendar — basic on free, full week and month views on Premium.

Reminders split the two as well. Todoist free includes automatic reminders for dated tasks, but custom and location reminders need Pro. TickTick free includes reminders at 2 per task, and Premium raises that to 5 per task, adds email reminders and a "constant reminder" that repeats until dismissed. Location reminders are reported as iOS-only on TickTick — verify before relying on them. Voice capture exists on both: Todoist's Ramble turns speech into tasks and its Task Assist AI (Pro) helps draft and organise, while TickTick's Premium added AI voice task creation and meeting transcription in 2026, per third-party reports — treat the TickTick AI features as reported, not vendor-verified.

Built-ins vs add-ons: TickTick's bundle against Todoist's ecosystem

TickTick's case is that it is an all-in-one: tasks, calendar, habits and Pomodoro in a single app, with one subscription and one interface. The Pomodoro timer comes with focus statistics, so you can see how many focused sessions you actually completed this week rather than how many you planned. The habit tracker runs streaks with statistics — 5 habits free, 299 on Premium — and the countdowns (5 free, 299 Premium) keep deadlines visible. The Eisenhower Matrix sorts tasks by urgency and importance, which is a genuinely useful view for a personal system, and the full calendar on Premium shows day, week and month views with task durations.

Todoist's case is the opposite philosophy: do one thing — task capture and organisation — better than anyone, and let other apps do the rest. That is a defensible design, and it is also a subscription bill. To match TickTick's bundle you need a habit app, a focus timer and a calendar app, and while the calendar is usually free, the other two are not. The trade is real: TickTick gives you everything in one place with a denser, busier interface that some users find cluttered; Todoist gives you a cleaner list and asks you to assemble the rest yourself.

One asymmetry matters more than either list: TickTick's built-ins work on the free plan, while Todoist's calendar layout, task durations and custom reminders are all Pro features. The free-plan walls: the exact limit you will hit first

Both free plans are genuinely usable, which is why this comparison is worth having — but each has a wall, and the wall you hit first decides whether free is a home or a trial.

Todoist free stops at 5 projects. Work, Home, Health, Money and one side project is already five, and the sixth project is the moment free stops working. The other free limits are 3 custom filter views, 5 people per personal project, 1 week of activity history, 5 MB file uploads, and 10 Ramble voice-capture sessions a month. One free team at a time is allowed, with up to 5 active team projects and 5 guests. Every plan, free or paid, caps at 300 active tasks per project and 20 sections per project — a ceiling most personal users never touch.

TickTick free stops on depth, not breadth. Nine lists sounds generous until the limits inside each list appear: 99 tasks per list, 19 checklist items per task, 2 reminders per task, 5 habits, 5 countdowns, 1 attachment per day, and 2 members on a shared list. The basic calendar is included, but the full week and month views are Premium. A user with six habits — exercise, reading, water, sleep, language, meditation — is already over the free cap on day one, and a task that needs three reminders hits the wall in week one.

Which wall is worse depends on how you work. If you organise by area of life, Todoist's 5-project cap bites first. If you track habits and set multiple reminders, TickTick's depth caps bite first. The honest advice: count your projects and your habits before you choose, because the number that exceeds the cap is the number that decides the upgrade — and the upgrade is $60 a year on one side and $49.99 on the other.

Teams, and switching later

Neither app is a team project tool, and the articles that pretend otherwise are doing their readers a disservice. TickTick has no team plan at all: shared lists cap at 29 members on Premium, and there is no workspace, no roles, no admin controls and no per-seat billing. Todoist Business exists — 500 team projects, roles, centralised billing, up to 1,000 members — but a personal project still caps at 5 collaborators on every plan, and the free team is limited to 5 team projects and 5 guests. If your household or small team needs a shared workspace with real permissions, neither of these is the answer; our Notion vs Asana comparison and our Asana vs Monday vs ClickUp guide cover tools built for that job.

Switching between the two is possible and unglamorous. Both export and import task lists, including CSV options, but nobody in the comparisons read for this article tested migration fidelity — recurring tasks, attachments, comments and reminders do not all survive a move intact, and every comparison either asserts "easy to switch" or says nothing. Treat migration as untested: export a test project first, check what arrives, and only then move the whole system. The switching cost is low in money and real in time, which is why the right choice on day one matters more than the price gap suggests.

The verdict, restated with the numbers attached: TickTick Premium at $49.99 a year is the better value for a solo user who wants one app — Pomodoro, habits, calendar and tasks, with the more generous free plan. Todoist Pro at $60 a year is the better tool for a heavy capturer who types tasks all day, lives in Gmail and Slack, and wants the deepest integrations. The $10 difference is not the decision. The decision is whether your bottleneck is capturing tasks fast or doing everything in one place — and that answer is yours, not the table's.

Frequently asked questions

Which is cheaper, Todoist or TickTick?

TickTick. TickTick Premium costs $49.99 a year on TickTick's website, against $60 a year for Todoist Pro billed annually ($5 a month). That is about $10 a year less, roughly 17%. TickTick also offers a $4.99 monthly option through the App Store, while Todoist Pro billed monthly is $7 a month ($84 a year).

Is TickTick Premium $35.99 or $49.99 a year?

The list price is $49.99 a year. Many comparison articles still quote $35.99, which was the old price — and TickTick has also run $35.99 as a promotional offer, so you may see it at checkout. Check the price on TickTick's upgrade page before you pay rather than trusting any single article, including this one.

Does Todoist have a Pomodoro timer or habit tracker?

No — not natively, at any price. Todoist has no Pomodoro timer, no habit tracker, no Eisenhower Matrix and no native calendar app. TickTick bundles all of them: a Pomodoro timer with focus statistics, a habit tracker with streaks (5 habits free, 299 on Premium), a full calendar and an Eisenhower Matrix. Todoist users who want those features need separate apps or paid integrations.

Can I use TickTick for a team?

Not really. TickTick has no team plan, no per-seat pricing, no workspace and no admin controls — Premium covers one user across all devices, and shared lists cap at 29 members. Todoist has a Business plan at $8 a user a month billed annually, with 500 team projects and roles. If you need a shared team workspace, look at tools built for it, such as Asana, ClickUp or Notion, rather than either of these two.

Can I migrate from Todoist to TickTick, or back?

Yes, both apps export and import task lists, including CSV options — but migration fidelity is untested: recurring tasks, attachments, comments and reminders may not all survive a move intact. Export a test project first, check what arrives, and only then move your whole system.

Conclusion

Todoist vs TickTick is a $10-a-year decision with a much bigger question underneath it: do you want the fastest capture tool in the category, or one app that does everything? TickTick Premium at $49.99 a year wins on value — Pomodoro, habits, calendar and the more generous free plan — and it is the pick for most solo users. Todoist Pro at $60 a year wins on capture speed and integrations, and it is the pick if you type twenty tasks a day and live in Gmail, Slack and Google Calendar. Count your projects and your habits, try both free plans for a week, and let the wall you hit first make the decision.
