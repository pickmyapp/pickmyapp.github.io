best SSL expiry monitoring tools for 64-day certificates (2026)

An expired SSL certificate is one of the most embarrassing outages a site can have: browsers flash a full-page warning, visitors bounce, and checkout stops. Prevention used to be easy, because 90-day certificates gave you weeks of slack. That changes with Let's Encrypt's new schedule — 64-day certificates by default from February 10, 2027, and 45-day certificates from February 16, 2028 (staging tests start October 14, 2026; full timeline in our Let's Encrypt 64-day certificates guide). A failed renewal discovered late leaves three weeks with 64-day certificates, barely two with 45-day ones. This guide reviews the best monitoring tools for the short-lived certificate era, compares costs, and shows you exactly what to set up.

Why 64-day certificates change monitoring

The schedule follows the CA/Browser Forum's decision to cap publicly trusted certificates at 47 days by March 2029:

  • May 13, 2026: the optional tlsserver ACME profile begins issuing 45-day certificates for early adopters and testing.
  • October 14, 2026: 64-day certificates arrive in Let's Encrypt's staging environment so administrators can test their automation.
  • February 10, 2027: the default classic profile switches to 64-day certificates, with domain authorization reuse dropping to 10 days.
  • February 16, 2028: the default moves to 45-day certificates, with authorization reuse collapsing to 7 hours.
  • The last 90-day certificates are expected to expire on May 11, 2027.

Three consequences matter. First, renewals happen twice as often — twice as many chances to fail. Second, the authorization reuse window shrinks (30 days → 10 days → 7 hours), making failed renewals slower to recover. Third, manual renewal becomes impractical. Let's Encrypt's guidance: renew at roughly two-thirds of the certificate's lifetime (around day 42 of 64). Our Certbot auto-renewal guide covers automation; this article covers the safety net.

Quick fact: shorter certificate lifetimes are a deliberate security improvement: they narrow the window for abused keys and make revocation less critical. The price is that everyone now needs automation and monitoring.

What to monitor (and when to alert)

Expiry monitoring is not just "warn me before the date." A good setup watches four things:

  • Days until expiry. With 64-day certificates, use an escalating ladder: 21 days (warning), 14 days (urgent), 7 days (critical). With 45-day certificates, compress that to 14, 7, and 3 days.
  • Certificate chain and validity. A renewed certificate with the wrong intermediate chain still breaks the site — check the certificate is actually valid, not just non-expired.
  • All domains and subdomains. The main site renews fine; the forgotten api.yoursite.com expires. Monitor every hostname your visitors touch.
  • Renewal success itself. Watch that the "not after" date advances after renewal day — if it does not, the renewal failed silently.

Route alerts where a human will act — email minimum, plus a chat message and mobile push. For client work, add the client's technical contact, and test the path once by lowering a threshold temporarily.

The 8 best SSL expiry monitoring tools

We rated each tool on SSL features, check frequency, alert channels, free-plan generosity, and price. Here is how they land.

1. UptimeRobot — cheapest way to cover many domains

UptimeRobot is the value pick: every plan — including free — includes SSL and domain expiry monitoring, with 50 monitors checked every 5 minutes on the free tier. The caveat: since late 2024, the free plan is personal, non-commercial use only. Businesses need a paid plan: Solo from roughly $9/month billed annually for 1-minute checks, Team from ~$38/month (100 monitors), Enterprise from ~$69/month (30-second checks). Paid tiers add Slack/Teams alerts, webhooks, Zapier, and PagerDuty; SMS and voice calls cost extra credits on all plans.

Best for: covering many domains cheaply with SSL expiry plus basic uptime in one tool.

2. Better Stack — best free plan for serious monitoring

Better Stack (formerly Better Uptime) has the most generous free tier here: 10 monitors checked every 3 minutes, hourly SSL expiry checks, a public status page, and on-call style alert routing — all for $0. Its incident management is the differentiator: you define the first alert, the escalation, and the incident communication. SSL checks on all plans have customizable expiry thresholds, with email, Slack, Teams, phone, SMS, PagerDuty, and webhook alerts. Paid team plans start higher than most rivals here (2026 reviews place the team tier around $85/month — confirm the current list).

Best for: small teams that want real incident management, not just pings, without paying for it.

3. StatusCake — solid all-rounder with generous SMS

StatusCake's free plan includes 10 uptime monitors (5-minute checks) and 1 SSL monitor checked daily — enough for your most important certificate. Superior (about €19.99/month) raises that to 100 uptime monitors at 1-minute intervals and 50 SSL monitors every 30 minutes; Business (about €69.99/month) goes to 300 monitors and 100 SSL monitors every 10 minutes. It offers 14+ notification channels — including 75 free SMS credits per month on the free plan — from 43 probe locations in 30 countries. The daily SSL interval on free is its main weakness for the 45-day future; pair it with a renewal-hook alert as backup.

Best for: teams that want SMS alerts without paying for credits.

4. Pingdom — the old reliable, but no free plan

Pingdom (part of SolarWinds) has monitored sites since 2007. It does SSL well: automatic expiry alerts, 1-minute checks on paid tiers, transaction monitoring for multi-step user flows, and page speed plus real user monitoring (RUM) as companions. The dealbreaker: there is no free plan — only a 14- to 30-day trial. Entry pricing sits around $10–15/month for 10 checks, with transaction monitoring and RUM as add-ons. It makes sense if you already pay for it and value checkout-flow testing; it is hard to justify as a new purchase purely for SSL expiry when free tools cover the core need.

Best for: existing Pingdom users and teams wanting uptime, transaction checks, and RUM in one place.

5. Site24x7 — the most complete free plan

Site24x7's free plan is the most complete here: 1-minute check intervals, SSL certificate monitoring, domain expiry tracking, and 100+ free diagnostic tools — no credit card required. Paid plans start at just $9/month, adding 130+ locations, AI anomaly detection, and full infrastructure monitoring from one console. SSL expiry sits alongside server, APM, and log monitoring — good if you want one dashboard for the whole stack, though the interface is denser than UptimeRobot's.

Best for: teams wanting SSL monitoring bundled with a broader infrastructure platform.

6. Oh Dear — the agency favorite, all features on every plan

Oh Dear unlocks every feature on every plan — uptime, SSL, DNS, broken-link crawling, cron monitoring, status pages, SSO, and unlimited users. Only the site count scales: Solo €15/month (2 sites), Freelance €49/month (10), Studio €99/month (25), Agency €149/month (50), with two months free on annual billing. Certificate monitoring verifies expiry dates and alerts on changes, with every alert confirmed from a second location — a real false-alarm killer. One caveat: reviews note its documentation is inconsistent about default SSL alert timing (30 days per marketing, 14/7 days per technical docs), so verify the timing on your plan.

Best for: agencies and freelancers managing client portfolios who want one bill and every feature unlocked.

7. Datadog — for teams already inside the Datadog ecosystem

Datadog's synthetic monitoring includes SSL certificate checks alongside API and browser tests from global locations, integrated with its APM, logs, and dashboards. Pricing is usage-based: roughly $5 per 10,000 API test runs and $12 per 1,000 browser test runs on an annual commitment, with a limited free tier. The catch: every check from every location burns runs, so costs compound fast — a mid-size setup can reach hundreds of dollars per month. Only choose Datadog if you already live in Datadog; buying it standalone for certificate expiry is like renting a warehouse to store a bicycle.

Best for: engineering teams already running Datadog who want expiry checks in existing dashboards.

8. Uptime Kuma — free and self-hosted, you own it

Uptime Kuma is the open-source outlier: free, self-hosted, unlimited monitors, and automatic SSL expiry monitoring with 90+ notification services. It runs on your own server — even a $5/month VPS — with no per-monitor costs and no commercial-use restrictions. The tradeoff is ownership: you maintain the server, the updates, and the alerts about the monitor itself (a dead monitor monitors nothing). If you already run a VPS you manage yourself, Kuma is the cheapest serious option.

Best for: developers who already run a VPS and want unlimited, private monitoring at near-zero cost.

Free vs paid: what you actually get

For SSL expiry specifically, free plans are surprisingly capable — a daily SSL check is plenty when certificates last 64 days. What matters is the rest of the package:

  • Stay free if: under 50 domains and email or app alerts suffice. Better Stack and Site24x7 are the strongest free options for business use; UptimeRobot's free plan is personal projects only.
  • Pay when: you manage client sites, need 1-minute uptime checks, want SMS/phone escalation, or need webhooks for your on-call rotation.
  • The sweet spot: most small businesses land on Site24x7's free plan or UptimeRobot Solo (~$9/month) and invest the savings in reliable renewal automation.

Setting up expiry monitoring in 6 steps

The setup is nearly identical in every tool:

  • 1. Inventory every certificate. List every public hostname — main domain, www, API subdomains, staging, client sites. The certificates that expire are always the ones you forgot.
  • 2. Add one SSL monitor per hostname. Most tools auto-detect the certificate when you add an HTTPS monitor. Where a dedicated SSL monitor type exists (StatusCake, Datadog), use it — it checks the chain and issuer, not just the date.
  • 3. Set escalating thresholds. 21 / 14 / 7 days for 64-day certificates (14 / 7 / 3 for 45-day). The first threshold should fire before your renewal automation's latest safe date.
  • 4. Route alerts to two places. A chat channel (Slack/Teams) for visibility and a mobile push or email for when you are away from the desk. For client sites, add the client's technical contact.
  • 5. Cover the domain too. Enable domain expiry alerts while you are there — a lapsed registration takes down everything, SSL included. UptimeRobot, Site24x7, and Oh Dear all include this.
  • 6. Test the alert path. Set one threshold to 90 days, confirm every channel fires, then set it back.

Finally, pair monitoring with automation: our Certbot auto-renewal guide covers the renewal side, and our cheap WordPress hosting guide and Cloudways vs SiteGround for agencies cover which hosts automate TLS well.

Frequently asked questions

Do I still need SSL expiry monitoring if my certificates renew automatically?

Yes. Automation fails silently: a broken cron job, an expired DNS API token, or a rate limit can all stop renewals. With 64-day certificates there are only about 42 days between the last safe renewal point and expiry, so a late-discovered failure leaves almost no buffer. Monitoring is the seatbelt over the airbag.

How many days before expiry should SSL alerts fire?

For 64-day certificates, set alerts at 21, 14, and 7 days remaining. Healthy renewals happen around day 42, so a 21-day alert means renewal already missed its window. For 45-day certificates, compress that ladder to 14, 7, and 3 days.

Can free monitoring tools watch SSL certificate expiry?

Yes. UptimeRobot's free plan includes SSL and domain expiry monitoring (50 monitors, but non-commercial use only since late 2024). Better Stack's free tier covers 10 monitors with 3-minute checks and SSL alerts, and Site24x7's free plan includes SSL and domain expiry tracking with 1-minute checks.

Does UptimeRobot monitor SSL certificates on the free plan?

Yes — SSL and domain expiry monitoring are included on every UptimeRobot plan, including the free one (50 monitors, 5-minute checks). The catch: the free plan is personal, non-commercial use only, so businesses need a paid plan starting at around $9 per month.

When do 64-day certificates actually start?

Let's Encrypt starts issuing 64-day certificates in staging on October 14, 2026; the default classic ACME profile switches to 64 days on February 10, 2027. The last 90-day certificates expire around May 11, 2027, and the default moves to 45 days on February 16, 2028.

The 64-day era makes expiry monitoring a baseline requirement, not a nice-to-have. Pick a tool today, inventory your hostnames, set escalating alerts at 21/14/7 days, and verify your renewal automation handles the shorter cycle. Your future self — the one not woken up by a browser warning page — will be grateful.
