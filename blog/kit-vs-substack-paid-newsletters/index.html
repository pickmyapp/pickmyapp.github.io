Kit vs Substack for Paid Newsletters: Which Pays Creators More? (2026)

  • Email Marketing
  • Published Nov 4, 2026
  • Updated: November 2026
  • 11 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

For a paid newsletter, Substack is the better starting point and Kit is the better business once you are earning. Substack costs nothing to start, needs no setup, and brings readers through its app and recommendation network. Kit charges a flat monthly fee instead of a percentage, so once paid revenue passes a few hundred dollars a month, Kit keeps more money in your pocket — about $315 a month more at 500 paid subscribers paying $8 a month. Start on Substack to find readers; move to Kit when the 10% fee starts to sting.

Kit and Substack are the two platforms paid newsletter writers most often shortlist, but they charge for opposite things. Substack takes a percentage of everything you earn and charges no monthly fee. Kit charges a flat monthly fee based on your list size and takes only a small transaction fee on sales. That single difference decides which one pays you more, and the answer flips as your newsletter grows. This guide compares pricing, features, audience ownership, and real earnings at realistic subscriber counts, using vendor pricing and fee schedules checked in November 2026. If you are still deciding between free newsletter tools, read our Substack vs beehiiv for free newsletters first.

What is Kit?

Kit, formerly ConvertKit, is an email marketing platform built for creators who sell things: newsletters, digital products, and courses. Launched in 2013 as ConvertKit, it rebranded to Kit in 2024. Its core tools are a visual automation builder, email sequences, subscriber tagging and segmentation, landing pages, forms, and built-in commerce for selling products and paid subscriptions directly from your emails.

Kit prices by total subscriber count, not by how much you earn. Its free plan covers up to 10,000 subscribers with unlimited broadcasts, landing pages, and forms, which is unusually generous. Paid plans add automations, sequences, and growth tools, and Kit says there are no sending limits on any plan. Kit bills only confirmed subscribers, so unconfirmed signups do not count against your tier.

What is Substack?

Substack is a publishing platform that combines a newsletter, a blog-style publication page, and a social network in one place. Writers publish posts that go out by email and appear on the web, readers can follow free or pay for a subscription, and the Substack app and Notes feed surface your work to readers who have never heard of you. There are no tiers, no feature gates, and no monthly fee at any list size.

Substack makes its money one way only: it takes 10% of every paid subscription payment. Card processing through Stripe comes on top of that. That simplicity is the whole pitch. You never pick a plan, never worry about subscriber-count pricing, and never pay anything while your publication is free. The trade-off is that the 10% never goes away, no matter how large you get.

How do Kit and Substack pricing compare?

The two pricing models reward different stages of a newsletter. Substack is free until you earn, then takes a tenth of everything forever. Kit is free up to 10,000 subscribers, then charges a flat fee that stays the same whether you earn $100 or $10,000 a month. The table below uses prices checked in November 2026.

Why the billing unit matters

Kit bills on total subscribers; Substack bills on paid revenue. A newsletter with 5,000 free readers and 100 paying readers pays Kit at the 5,000-subscriber tier ($89 a month for Creator) but pays Substack only on the 100 paying readers. Conversely, a newsletter with 1,000 subscribers who mostly pay costs Kit only $39 a month while Substack takes 10% of a much larger revenue number. Neither model is universally cheaper. The crossover depends on your paid conversion rate, which is why the earnings math below matters more than the headline prices.

Quick fact: On a $10 monthly subscription, Substack keeps $1.00 and Stripe keeps about 66 cents, so you net about $8.34. On a $50 annual subscription you net about $42.90, because the 30-cent flat fee is charged once instead of twelve times. Annual plans are worth pushing on either platform.

How do Kit and Substack compare on features?

Automation and email tools

Kit is a full email marketing platform, and it shows. The Creator plan includes unlimited visual automations and email sequences, so you can run a welcome series, a course drip, and a win-back flow at the same time. You also get tagging and segmentation, A/B subject-line tests, subscriber polls, and integrations with the rest of your stack. Substack has no real automation: no sequences, no tagging, no drip campaigns. It sends your posts and little else. If your newsletter is also your sales funnel, Kit wins this category outright. See our Mailchimp vs ConvertKit comparison for creators for how Kit's automation stacks up against a general email tool.

Discovery and growth

Substack wins here, and it is not close. Your publication lives inside a network: readers discover you through recommendations, the Substack app, Notes, and leaderboards, and paid subscribers can bring friends with them. Kit has its own growth tools — a recommendation network, including paid recommendations, and a referral system on the Pro plan — but they operate inside email, not inside a social feed. Kit takes 23.5% of paid recommendation and sponsorship earnings, so that growth channel is not free either. For a brand-new writer with no audience, Substack's built-in discovery is often worth more than the fee difference.

Selling beyond subscriptions

Kit is the stronger store. You can sell digital products, courses, and one-off purchases alongside subscriptions, all through the same checkout, at the same 3.5% + 30-cent fee. Substack added shops and other monetization options over time, but paid subscriptions remain its center of gravity, and everything still runs through its 10% cut. If your business plan includes a course launch or a product drop, Kit's commerce tools are the better fit.

Writing and publishing experience

Substack's editor is simpler and its publication pages look like a real magazine out of the box, with archives, about pages, and comments built in. Kit's emails are strong, but its public-facing pages are landing pages and forms, not a publication home. Writers who care about the reading experience and community discussion usually prefer Substack's front end, even if they later move their list to Kit.

Who owns your audience, and can you leave?

Both platforms let you export your email list, and you should do it regularly wherever you publish. That is the good news. The hard part is paid subscribers. Free subscribers move as a CSV file in either direction. Paid subscribers generally have to subscribe again on the new platform, which means asking people who already pay you to re-enter their payment details — expect some churn at that step.

There is also a trap worth knowing before you move off Substack. Migration guides warn that if you move paid subscribers while keeping the same Stripe account Substack used, Substack may continue taking its 10% on those renewals. Check your Stripe setup and read the migration terms carefully before you start, and time the move between billing cycles so no reader is charged twice. Moving from Kit to Substack is simpler mechanically, since you are moving toward the percentage model, but you give up Kit's automations and segmentation in the process.

Quick fact: Kit bills only confirmed subscribers, so unconfirmed signups never inflate your tier. On Substack there is no tier to inflate, but inactive free subscribers still count toward nothing — you only ever pay when someone pays you.

Which should you choose: Kit or Substack?

Choose Substack if

  • You are starting from zero and need readers, not just email tools.
  • You want to publish today with no setup, no plan choice, and no monthly bill.
  • Your paid revenue is under a few hundred dollars a month, so the 10% fee is small in absolute terms.
  • You value the publication website, comments, and app discovery over automation.

Choose Kit if

  • Your paid revenue is already a few hundred dollars a month or more.
  • You sell products or courses alongside your newsletter.
  • You need welcome sequences, tagging, segmentation, and real automation.
  • You want your costs to stay flat as your revenue grows.

The pragmatic path for many writers is both, in sequence: build on Substack while discovery matters most, then move the list to Kit when the monthly 10% check exceeds what a flat plan would cost. At the 500-paid-subscriber example above, that move is worth about $315 a month. For a pricing-focused comparison at a larger list, see our MailerLite vs Kit pricing at 25,000 subscribers.

Frequently asked questions

Is Substack really free?

Yes, publishing on Substack is free at any list size. You pay nothing until readers pay you. Once you turn on paid subscriptions, Substack takes 10% of each payment and Stripe takes card processing fees on top, so a free publication costs nothing but a paid one gives up roughly 14% to 20% of each payment in fees.

What percentage does Substack take from paid subscriptions?

Substack takes a flat 10% of every paid subscription payment, and it never steps down as you grow. Stripe adds about 2.9% plus 30 cents per payment and a 0.7% recurring billing fee. On a $10 monthly subscription you keep about $8.34; on a $50 annual subscription you keep about $42.90.

Is Kit free for paid newsletters?

Kit's free plan covers up to 10,000 subscribers with unlimited broadcasts, and you can sell paid subscriptions on it, paying only Kit's 3.5% plus 30 cents per transaction, which includes card processing. The catch is automation: the free plan's automation allowance has shrunk over time, and a welcome series or any real automation sequence requires the Creator plan, from $39 a month at 1,000 subscribers.

Which pays more at 500 paid subscribers?

Kit, by a clear margin. At 500 paid subscribers paying $8 a month ($4,000 gross), Substack's fees run about $694 a month, leaving about $3,306. On Kit's Creator plan at 5,000 total subscribers ($89 a month) plus 3.5% plus 30 cents per payment ($290), total cost is about $379, leaving about $3,621. That is roughly $315 more per month in the creator's pocket.

Can I move my paid subscribers from Substack to Kit?

Free subscribers move easily: export your email list from Substack as a CSV and import it into Kit. Paid subscribers are harder. They generally have to subscribe again on the new platform, and migration guides warn that moving paid subscribers on the same Stripe account can leave Substack still taking its 10% on those renewals. Plan the move between billing cycles and tell paying readers exactly what to do.

Conclusion

Substack is the best place to start a paid newsletter: free, instant, and plugged into a discovery network that can bring you your first paying readers. Kit is the best place to run a paid newsletter once it earns real money: a flat monthly fee, full automation, and commerce tools that keep more of every dollar with you. The crossover comes earlier than most writers expect — somewhere in the low hundreds of dollars a month in paid revenue — so treat Substack as a launchpad, not a forever home, and re-run the math when your paid count crosses 100.
