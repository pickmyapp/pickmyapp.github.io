Asana free vs paid: what the limits really mean (2026)

  • Project Management
  • Published Oct 15, 2026
  • Updated: October 2026
  • 10 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

Asana's free Personal plan is capped at 2 users — it is a plan for an individual plus a colleague, not a team. You get unlimited tasks and projects with list, board and calendar views, but no Timeline, no automations, no custom fields and no dashboards. Starter at $10.99 per user per month billed annually ($13.49 month to month) adds all of those, and it is the tier most small teams actually land on. Advanced at $24.99 per user per month ($30.49 monthly) adds portfolios, goals, workload and time tracking — buy it when you manage many projects across people, not before.

Asana's pricing page looks simple — a free plan, then three paid steps — but the limits that matter are not the ones in the headline. The free Personal plan quietly caps you at two users, the Timeline view that most teams consider basic is a paid feature, and portfolios, goals and time tracking sit a full tier above where many teams expect them. If you are comparing project tools more broadly, our Asana vs Monday vs ClickUp comparison for small teams puts these limits next to the competition. Here is what each Asana tier actually gives you, in practical terms, based on the published pricing page and plan documentation checked in October 2026.

The two things to internalize before reading further: the free plan is genuinely capped at two people, and there is no single-user paid plan — paid seats start at two.

What the free Personal plan actually gives you

The free plan is more generous than it looks on the pricing page, and less generous than most people assume. You get unlimited tasks, projects, messages and activity logs, plus list, board and calendar views, assignees, due dates, comments, subtasks and the mobile app. File attachments are capped at 100 MB per file, and you get access to 100+ free integrations including Slack and Google Drive. For a freelancer and one collaborator tracking work with due dates, that is a genuinely usable, free-forever setup — no credit card required.

But read the word "Personal" literally. The current pricing page caps the plan at 2 users, and several independent reviewers confirm this is the constraint that catches teams out: if you tried Asana years ago on a free team of ten, that team size no longer qualifies. Accounts created before the plan changes may keep their older, higher limit, but any new workspace is a two-person plan. Guests can be invited to individual projects without taking up a seat, which softens the cap slightly — clients and contractors can view or comment on specific projects for free — but they cannot work across the whole workspace.

Compared with rivals, the free tier is middle of the pack. ClickUp's free plan, for example, includes unlimited members and even a Gantt view, while Monday.com's free individual plan caps at 2 seats and 3 boards. The limits that bite first

Free-plan limits do not all hurt equally. Here is the order in which they tend to actually stop real teams, from first pain to last.

1. The 2-user cap

This is the limit almost every growing team hits first, and it is a hard wall: the third person forces everyone onto a paid plan. There is no middle tier for a team of three or four — you pay Starter's per-seat rate for the whole workspace. If you are a team of three deciding whether Asana is "free," it is not.

2. No Timeline or Gantt view

The Timeline view — the horizontal bar chart that shows how tasks connect over time — is the feature most teams assume is basic. On Personal, it does not exist. You cannot visualize a project schedule, and you cannot see how a slipped deadline cascades. Teams running launches, campaigns or client projects usually hit this within weeks. This is also why many teams compare Asana with more visual alternatives; see our Notion vs Asana breakdown for project tracking for that angle.

3. No automations (rules)

Personal has no rules engine: no "when a task moves to Done, assign it to the reviewer," no automatic due-date assignment, no forms that create tasks with the right fields. Every one of those is manual work, and on a busy team it is exactly the busywork that eats an hour a day. Starter's headline feature is arguably not the Timeline — it is unlimited automations.

4. No custom fields, forms or dashboards

Without custom fields you cannot tag tasks with priority, budget, client or channel. Without forms you cannot turn intake requests into properly structured tasks. Without dashboards you cannot see progress across projects in one place. Together, these three are what separate "a shared to-do list" from "a project management system," and they are all paid.

5. No admin console, portfolios or goals

These bite later, but they bite hard. Without an admin console there is no central member management or security control. Portfolios and goals — the views that answer "how are all our projects doing?" and "are we on track for the quarter?" — sit on Advanced, two tiers up from free. A manager overseeing several workstreams will feel this gap the moment the team has more than a few projects running.

Quick fact: Asana sells seats in brackets above five users, so a team of six may pay for ten seats and a team of twelve for fifteen. The bracket structure has shifted over time, so treat the per-seat math below as a floor and confirm the actual seat count at checkout.

What Starter adds, and what it really costs

Starter at $10.99 per user per month billed annually ($13.49 month to month) is the tier most small teams land on, and it is where Asana becomes a real project management tool. You get the Timeline and Gantt views, reporting dashboards, custom fields, forms with branching, the workflow builder with unlimited automations, an admin console, custom templates, and AI Studio Basic with 50,000 AI credits. There is no seat limit, and guests remain free and unlimited.

The practical effect: a 5-person marketing team on Starter can plan a campaign on a Timeline, intake requests through a form that auto-assigns them, route approvals with rules, and report progress on a dashboard — none of which is possible on Personal. At $10.99 per user, that team pays about $55 a month, or $659 a year on annual billing. Month-to-month billing for the same team runs about $67 a month, so the annual commitment saves roughly 18%.

What Starter does not give you: portfolios, goals, workload management, approvals and proofing, native time tracking, or the Salesforce/Tableau/Power BI integrations. Several reviewers flag this as the number people get wrong — they budget for Starter and then discover the feature they actually needed was Advanced. If any of those sound like your team, read the next section before you buy.

What Advanced adds — and who needs it

Advanced costs $24.99 per user per month billed annually ($30.49 month to month) — roughly double Starter — and it is aimed at a different buyer. The additions are portfolios (group projects into one view), goals (connect work to company objectives), workload and universal workload (see who is overloaded across projects), approvals and proofing, native time tracking, formulas, and scaled integrations including Salesforce, Tableau and Power BI, with more AI Studio credits.

The honest test for Advanced is a question: do you manage projects or a portfolio of projects? A single team running one product launch, one campaign or one client engagement does not need Advanced — Starter's dashboards are enough. A manager overseeing five workstreams across two departments, or an agency juggling twenty client projects, needs portfolios and workload to see status and capacity without chasing people. That is who the extra $14 per seat per month is for. Our ClickUp pricing breakdown is worth a look if Advanced's price makes you want to comparison-shop before committing.

Two caveats that move these numbers. First, the seat brackets mentioned earlier: above five users, Asana sells seats in groups, so your headcount may buy more seats than you have people — check the checkout, not the calculator. Second, several security and resource features buyers assume are included — SAML SSO and SCIM provisioning, capacity planning, compliance controls — are Enterprise or paid add-ons, not part of Starter or Advanced. If your IT team requires SSO, you are having a sales conversation, not a self-serve signup.

When should you actually upgrade?

Stay on Personal if

  • You are an individual, a freelancer, or a pair working with one collaborator.
  • Lists, boards and a calendar cover everything you track.
  • Nobody needs to see a project schedule, a workload view or a dashboard.
  • Manual task management is not yet costing you real time each week.

Upgrade to Starter if

  • A third person needs in — the 2-user cap forces the decision.
  • You need a Timeline to plan and communicate a schedule.
  • Repetitive routing and assignment work is eating hours every week.
  • You need forms for intake, custom fields for tracking, or dashboards for reporting.

And consider Advanced only when you are managing across projects, not within one: portfolios for the manager with five workstreams, goals for the team that reports on objectives, workload for the lead who needs to see who is drowning. If none of those describe you, Advanced is $14 per seat per month of features you will open once and forget.

The upgrade decision that matters most is the first one, because it is the biggest relative jump — from $0 to $10.99 per seat. A useful rule: upgrade when the missing feature costs you more than the seats. If the absence of automations costs your team three hours a week, Starter pays for itself at any team size. If you are upgrading "just in case," wait until the pain is real — the free tier is genuinely good at what it does, and Asana will happily take your money whenever you are ready.

Frequently asked questions

How many people can use Asana for free?

Asana's current pricing page caps the free Personal plan at 2 users. Older accounts created before the plan rename may keep their previous, higher limit, but any new workspace set up today is limited to two people on the free tier. Guests can be invited to individual projects without taking up a seat.

What is missing from the free Asana plan?

The free Personal plan omits the features most teams eventually need: the Timeline and Gantt views, task dependencies, automations (rules), custom fields, forms, reporting dashboards, milestones, portfolios and any admin console. You get unlimited tasks and projects with list, board and calendar views, plus 100 MB file attachments.

Is Asana Starter worth it for a small team?

Yes, when you need what it adds: Timeline and Gantt views, custom fields, forms, rules-based automation, dashboards and an admin console, at $10.99 per user per month billed annually ($13.49 month to month). For a 5-user team that is about $55 a month. It is worth it the moment missing timelines or automations cost your team more time than the seats cost.

What does Asana Advanced add over Starter?

Advanced adds portfolios, goals, workload management, approvals and proofing, native time tracking, formulas and deeper integrations (Salesforce, Tableau, Power BI). It costs $24.99 per user per month billed annually, roughly double Starter. It is for teams managing many projects across people or departments, not for a single team running one project.

How much does Asana cost for a 15-person team?

On Starter, 15 users cost about $165 per month billed annually, or $1,979 per year. On Advanced, the same team costs about $375 per month, or $4,498 per year. Note that Asana sells seats in brackets above five users, so check the checkout for how many seats your headcount actually buys.

Conclusion

Asana's free Personal plan is a genuinely good task manager for one or two people — and a trap for everyone else. The 2-user cap, the missing Timeline and the absence of automations mean most real teams land on Starter at $10.99 per user per month, which is where Asana earns its reputation. Advanced's portfolios and workload are excellent, but only if you manage across projects rather than within one. Price the tier you will end up on, not the one you sign up for, and upgrade when the missing feature costs more than the seats.
