MailerLite vs Kit pricing at 25k subscribers compared

  • Email Marketing
  • Published Oct 16, 2026
  • Updated: October 2026
  • 10 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

At exactly 25,000 subscribers in October 2026, MailerLite costs $179/month on the Comfort plan or $239/month on Power, while Kit (formerly ConvertKit) costs $199/month on Creator or $279/month on Creator Pro — all monthly billing. MailerLite is the cheaper option at every comparable tier, but the gap is small: just $20/month between Comfort and Creator, or about $59 a year on annual billing. The real decision comes down to features: Kit's unlimited sends and creator-monetization tools versus MailerLite's active-subscriber-only billing and lower base price.

The exact numbers at 25,000 subscribers

Pricing for both platforms changed significantly in the last year, so forget anything you read before mid-2026. MailerLite overhauled its plans on June 16, 2026: the old Growing Business and Advanced plans were renamed to Comfort and Power, prices went up, and the free plan shrank. Kit (the platform formerly known as ConvertKit) repriced in September 2025 with notably higher tiers at scale. Here is where both stand today, verified against current pricing data in October 2026.

MailerLite wins on sticker price at both tiers: $20/month cheaper at the standard level, $40/month cheaper at the top level. Annual billing narrows things further — MailerLite's 10% annual discount and Kit's roughly 16% annual discount bring the standard-tier gap down to about $4.90 a month, or roughly $59 over a full year. When two platforms land within sixty dollars a year of each other, price stops being the deciding factor and features take over.

Tier by tier: where the price crossover happens

The 25,000-subscriber mark is interesting because it's roughly where the historical pricing story flips. At small list sizes, MailerLite has long been dramatically cheaper; as lists grow into the tens of thousands, Kit's pricing curve steepens and the gap compresses. Here's how the standard tiers compare as you scale:

The "crossover" is in value, not price: above roughly 10,000 subscribers, Kit's creator-focused features start to justify its premium for the right kind of sender.

One more pricing nuance worth knowing: both platforms auto-tier you upward as your list grows, so a list hovering near 25,000 that tips over the threshold moves to the next price band automatically. If your list oscillates around a tier boundary because of seasonal signups and churn, budget for the higher band.

The active-subscriber detail that changes the math

Here is the single most underappreciated line in this comparison: MailerLite bills only for active subscribers. Unsubscribed and bounced contacts don't count toward your tier, according to MailerLite's published pricing notes. At 25,000 total contacts with, say, 15% unsubscribed or bounced — a normal figure for an older list — you'd be billed closer to the 21,000-subscriber reality, and the tiered pricing means a meaningfully smaller list can slip into a lower band.

Kit prices by subscriber count without an equivalent active-only carve-out in its published pricing. That makes list hygiene a direct cost lever on MailerLite in a way it isn't on Kit: regularly purging bounced and chronically unengaged contacts doesn't just improve deliverability, it can literally hold your bill in a lower tier. For senders migrating with a large, aging list, run the numbers on active contacts, not total contacts, before comparing.

Send limits: capped versus unlimited

MailerLite Comfort includes a monthly email allowance that scales with your tier — roughly ten times your subscriber count per month based on the published tier table (for example, 100,000 emails per month at the 10,000-subscriber tier). For most senders that's plenty: a weekly newsletter to 25,000 subscribers is about 100,000 to 125,000 sends a month. But if you run daily sends plus triggered automations plus RSS campaigns, you can bump against the ceiling, and that's exactly the use case Power's unlimited sends (subject to fair use) is priced for.

Kit includes unlimited broadcasts and automations on both paid tiers with no send cap to manage. If your sending volume is high and unpredictable — daily content, behavioral triggers firing constantly, multiple segments — Kit removes an entire category of billing anxiety. This is the cleanest functional argument for paying Kit's $20/month premium at the standard tier: you never have to think about send volume again.

Paid newsletters and monetization

At 25,000 subscribers, many newsletters are revenue products, not just marketing channels. Both platforms support paid newsletters on their paid plans, but the economics differ. Kit does not take a transaction fee on paid newsletter revenue — you keep what Stripe (or your payment processor) leaves you. That's a genuine structural advantage for creators whose newsletter is the business: on $5,000 a month of subscription revenue, even a small platform cut would dwarf the $20–$40 pricing gap.

MailerLite supports selling digital products and paid newsletters on its paid plans, and our Kit vs Beehiiv comparison for paid newsletters digs deeper into monetization-first platform choice. If direct reader revenue is your model, evaluate the total take-home — platform fee plus payment processing — not just the subscription price.

The cost of switching at this scale

Migrating 25,000 subscribers isn't just an export-and-import job; it's automations to rebuild, forms to replace, templates to recreate, and a deliverability warm-up on the new platform's sending infrastructure. Kit softens this with free list migration on Creator and Creator Pro plans — they'll move your list for you. Factor in a week or two of parallel running, where you're paying for both platforms, plus the soft cost of rebuilding your five most complex automations from scratch.

Switching math at 25,000 subscribers: moving from Kit Creator ($199) to MailerLite Comfort ($179) saves $240 a year — less than the value of a single day of a marketer's time. Don't switch platforms to save $20 a month; switch because the feature set fits your next 12 months of growth.

For context on budget options at smaller scales, see our guides to the cheapest email marketing software for small businesses and the best free email marketing tools in 2026 — and note that MailerLite's free plan now covers only 250 subscribers after the June 2026 overhaul, while Kit's free Newsletter plan stretches to 10,000 subscribers but ships without automations.

Choose MailerLite if…

Price per subscriber is your primary metric, your list includes a meaningful share of inactive contacts (which MailerLite won't bill you for), your send volume fits comfortably inside Comfort's allowance, and you don't need subscriber scoring or a referral engine. Comfort at $179/month is simply the cheapest credible way to email 25,000 people in 2026.

Choose Kit if…

You send high, unpredictable volumes and want unlimited sends with zero cap anxiety, you're building a monetized newsletter where Kit's zero platform fee on paid subscriptions matters, or you need subscriber scoring and the referral system on Creator Pro to drive growth. The $20/month premium buys creative-monetization infrastructure, not just emails.

Frequently asked questions

Which is cheaper at 25,000 subscribers — MailerLite or Kit?

MailerLite. Comfort costs $179/month versus Kit Creator's $199/month, and Power costs $239/month versus Creator Pro's $279/month (monthly billing, October 2026). On annual billing the standard-tier gap shrinks to roughly $59 a year. For a broader pricing context, our Brevo vs Mailchimp comparison shows how both stack up against other volume-priced platforms.

Do MailerLite and Kit charge for unsubscribed or bounced contacts?

MailerLite bills only for active subscribers — unsubscribed and bounced contacts don't count toward your tier, which can keep a large list in a lower price band. Kit prices by subscriber count without a published active-only equivalent, so list hygiene is a direct cost lever on MailerLite in a way it isn't on Kit.

Can I use Kit's free plan at 25,000 subscribers?

No. Kit's free Newsletter plan caps at 10,000 subscribers and no longer includes automations, so a 25,000-subscriber sender needs a paid plan regardless. The free tier is useful for testing deliverability and the editor before committing, not for running a list of this size.

Which platform is better for paid newsletters at 25,000 subscribers?

Kit has the structural edge: it takes no transaction fee on paid newsletter revenue, so creators keep everything after payment processing. MailerLite supports paid newsletters on its paid plans too, but if reader revenue is your business model, Kit's zero platform cut can be worth far more than the subscription-price difference.

How hard is it to migrate 25,000 subscribers from MailerLite to Kit (or back)?

The contact move itself is straightforward — CSV export and import — but the real work is rebuilding automations, forms, and templates, plus warming up deliverability on the new infrastructure. Kit offers free list migration on its paid plans. Budget one to two weeks of parallel running and don't underestimate the automation rebuild; that's where migrations actually succeed or fail.

Conclusion

At 25,000 subscribers, MailerLite vs Kit is a $20-a-month question with a features-first answer. MailerLite Comfort at $179/month is the cheapest credible option, made cheaper still by active-subscriber-only billing — ideal for cost-conscious senders with normal weekly cadences. Kit Creator at $199/month justifies its premium with unlimited sends, unlimited automations, and a monetization stack (including zero platform fees on paid newsletters) built for creators turning subscribers into revenue. Pick the one whose strengths match what your list will look like twelve months from now, not just what it costs today.

A note from the author: I've seen too many senders switch email platforms to save the cost of a pizza night per month, then spend three weeks rebuilding automations. At 25,000 subscribers, your platform is infrastructure, not an expense line to optimize. Choose on features, migrate once, and get back to writing emails people open.
