What is Vokolla?

Vokolla is a self-service press release platform for businesses and brands that want to announce news online without hiring a PR agency. Based in Cheyenne, Wyoming, and founded by Richard Lee, it combines AI-assisted writing and editorial checks with distribution and a report of every published link. Its pitch is aimed squarely at the businesses that have historically skipped press releases entirely: founders, indie developers, and small software teams who have news worth sharing but no communications department and no agency retainer.

The platform works in seven languages — English, Chinese, Spanish, French, German, Japanese, and Korean — and its core product is the AI Press Release Agent, launched on October 23, 2026. Owners describe their news in a few sentences, or paste an existing draft, and the agent turns it into a release in plain, factual language. Crucially, the agent asks for anything it needs — a quote, a launch date, a location — rather than inventing facts to fill the gaps. Before submission, the release is checked again against the standards of the media package the owner selects, and the owner approves the final version. After publication, the owner receives an email at each stage and a report listing every page where the release appeared.

Two days after the agent launched, on October 25, 2026, Vokolla announced the connection that matters most to developers: the agent now works inside AI assistants and code editors through MCP. That is the feature this article is about, and it is what makes Vokolla different from a conventional wire service with an AI writing add-on.

How does the Claude, Cursor, and Gemini connection work?

The connection uses the Model Context Protocol (MCP), an open standard that lets AI assistants talk to outside services. Instead of opening a browser tab, copying text between tools, and reformatting it for a press release form, the developer works inside the assistant or editor they already have open. Setup takes a few minutes: add Vokolla's connector address to the assistant or editor, sign in to a Vokolla account, and approve access. Instructions are published on Vokolla's connector page.

Once connected, the workflow runs like this:

  • Draft from your own materials. Ask the assistant to draft a release from the project's description, README, or changelog — the documents that already describe what you built, in your own words.
  • Fill the gaps honestly. The agent asks for missing facts, such as a launch date, a location, or a quote, rather than inventing them. A release with a fabricated quote or date is worse than no release at all: editors reject it, and readers who check will not trust the next one.
  • Check against editorial requirements. The agent checks the draft against the requirements publishers apply, including a dateline, a factual first paragraph, and neutral language, and explains each issue it finds. Press releases that read like advertisements are the most common reason small announcements get rejected.
  • Save to your workspace. When the draft is ready, the assistant saves it to your Vokolla workspace. Nothing is ordered or charged from inside the conversation.
  • Confirm and pay on Vokolla. You open Vokolla, choose a publishing package, confirm the final version, and pay. Distribution only starts after you approve it.
  • Track publication. After submission, you can ask the assistant for the release's status, and you receive an email at each stage plus a report listing every page where the release appeared.

The design choice that matters here is the boundary between drafting and spending. The assistant can write and check, but it cannot order distribution or charge your card. For a small team handing an AI tool access to a company account, that boundary is the difference between a useful integration and a liability.

What does Vokolla cost?

Drafting and checking are available to anyone with a free Vokolla account; distribution is paid per release. The connection itself does not carry a separate fee in Vokolla's announcement. You pay only when you choose to publish, and you choose the package on Vokolla's site, not inside the chat.

Read that table with one honest caveat, which Vokolla itself states in its own materials: a package lists the outlets that may carry your release, and editors decide what they publish. The post-publication report shows where your release actually appeared. A $379 package does not guarantee a USA Today placement; it buys distribution to a list that includes USA Today, subject to editorial review. Any vendor that promises a specific outlet outright should be treated with suspicion, and Vokolla does not make that promise.

For context, a traditional PR agency retainer typically runs into four figures per month, and a single professionally written release from a freelancer can cost several hundred dollars before distribution. Against that baseline, $69 for a first release is cheap — but only if the release is genuinely newsworthy. Paying $69 to announce a minor version bump that no editor will run is still $69 wasted.

What does this mean for small dev teams?

For a small dev team, Vokolla's MCP connection removes the two excuses that usually kill a launch announcement: "we don't know how to write one" and "we don't have time to deal with it." The announcement becomes the last step of the same session that built the product, written from the same README and changelog the team already maintains. That matters because the launch window is short: a release published the week a product ships is news; the same release published two months later, after the team has moved on to the next feature, is an afterthought.

The typical launches Vokolla is built for map neatly onto what small teams actually ship: open-source releases, new apps and browser extensions, SaaS features, developer tools, and side projects ready for their first users. If you have ever shipped something, posted it to one social feed, watched it sink, and thought "we should have announced that properly," this is the gap Vokolla is aiming at.

There is also a quieter, longer-term reason a public announcement matters in 2026: AI search. When someone — or some assistant — checks whether a company is legitimate, it answers from what is public on the web. Vokolla cites a Muck Rack analysis from May 2026 finding that 84% of links cited by AI tools came from earned media: third-party editorial and news content, not a brand's own pages. A dated press release on third-party news sites is a public record that both people and machines can find. Your own blog post announcing the same launch does not carry the same weight, because it is self-published. Four to six substantive releases a year, tied to real events — a launch, a major customer, a partnership, original data — build a verifiable timeline for a company that otherwise exists only on its own website.

Treat a release as one launch channel among several: the announcement, the changelog, the demo, the community post. Vokolla's value is that it makes the announcement channel cheap enough and easy enough that a two-person team actually uses it.

What has not been independently verified?

As of October 26, 2026, every detailed source on Vokolla is Vokolla's own announcement, syndicated across press release sites, plus Vokolla's own blog posts. We could not find an independent review, a hands-on test of the MCP setup, or published data on how often releases actually get picked up by the named outlets. That does not mean the claims are false — the company, founder, contact details, and product pages are all public and consistent — but it does mean you should treat the following as vendor claims until independent coverage exists:

  • How smooth the MCP setup really is across Claude, Cursor, and Gemini, and whether all three behave identically.
  • How often releases on each tier actually appear on the headline outlets versus smaller syndication sites.
  • How strict the editorial checks are in practice, and how many drafts get sent back for revision.
  • Whether the traffic figures quoted for outlets (for example, Yahoo Finance at roughly 230 million visits a month) match independent measurement.

The practical way to handle this uncertainty is to start at the $69 tier with a genuinely newsworthy launch, read the publication report link by link, and only then decide whether a higher tier is worth it. A vendor's first order is a test, not a commitment.

The launch release checklist: what editors actually want

Whether you use Vokolla or write the release yourself, the same editorial requirements apply. Use this checklist before you submit anything:

  • A real dateline. City and date at the top, matching where the company is actually based.
  • A factual first paragraph. Who, what, when, and why it matters — in the first two sentences. No "thrilled to announce" throat-clearing.
  • Neutral language throughout. Describe what the product does, not how amazing it is. Superlatives ("revolutionary," "game-changing," "world's first") are the fastest route to rejection unless you can prove them.
  • One real quote. A quote from a named founder or lead that says something a press release cannot say in its own voice — why you built it, who it is for. Invented quotes are a reputational risk.
  • Concrete facts. A launch date, pricing, availability, and a link. Vague announcements ("coming soon," "exciting features") get ignored.
  • A boilerplate and a contact. Two or three sentences about the company and a real media contact who answers email.
  • No advertising copy. If a sentence would fit in a paid ad, rewrite it. Editors publish news, not ads.

If your announcement cannot pass this checklist, fix the announcement before you pay for distribution. Distribution amplifies what you submit; it does not improve it.

How does Vokolla compare with the alternatives?

For a small team, the realistic alternatives are a PR agency, a traditional wire service, or doing nothing. An agency buys expertise and relationships but costs a monthly retainer that dwarfs a $69 release, and most agencies will not take a two-person side project as a client. Traditional wire services offer broad distribution but were built for corporate communications teams, with pricing and interfaces to match; their AI writing help, where it exists, is usually a form on a website, not an agent inside the tool you build in. Doing nothing costs nothing and is what most small teams choose by default — which is exactly why their launches stay invisible.

Vokolla's distinctive bet is the workflow, not the distribution list: drafting and checking inside the builder's own tools, with payment and approval kept on Vokolla's site. If that workflow works as announced, it is a genuine improvement for its target audience. If the MCP setup turns out to be fiddly or the editorial checks turn out to be light, it is still a cheap wire service with a good writing assistant. Either way, the $69 test order is the way to find out.

Verdict

Vokolla is a real, newly launched option for small dev teams that want a launch announcement without an agency. The MCP connection to Claude, Cursor, and Gemini is genuinely new, the free drafting tier removes the cost of trying it, and the $69 entry package makes a first test cheap. Go in with eyes open: the detailed claims are, for now, the vendor's own, and a package lists outlets that may carry your release rather than guaranteeing placement. Draft from your real README and changelog, pass the editorial checklist above, start at the lowest tier, and judge it by the publication report you actually receive.
