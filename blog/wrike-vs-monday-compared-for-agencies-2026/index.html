Wrike vs Monday compared for agencies (2026)

  • Project Management
  • Published Nov 12, 2026
  • Updated: November 2026
  • 12 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

Wrike and Monday.com both run agency work, but they charge for different strengths. Wrike's paid plans start at Team $10 and Business $25 per user a month on annual billing, with no monthly prices published. Monday's paid plans start at Basic $9, Standard $12 and Pro $19 on annual billing ($12, $14 and $24 monthly), with a 3-seat minimum on every paid plan. For a 5-person agency at the working tier, Wrike Business costs $125 a month ($1,500 a year) against Monday Pro at $95 a month ($1,140 a year) — Monday is about 24% cheaper per seat. Wrike's clearest win is native proofing and approvals plus deeper resource management, both on Business. Monday's clearest win is client access: unlimited free read-only viewers from Basic and guest access from Standard, where Wrike charges for external users as paid seats.

Pricing at a glance — the two menus

The headline prices look close — $10 against $9 at the door, $25 against $19 at the working tier — but the two menus are built differently, and the differences decide the real bill. Wrike publishes annual prices only. Its pricing page states the amount shown is "priced per month and billed on an annual per-user basis", and its FAQ says Business and above are annual-subscription only. No monthly rate is published for any plan, so a team that wants month-to-month billing has no figure to budget against.

Wrike's Team at $10 covers 2 to 15 users, with interactive Gantt charts, shareable dashboards, calendars, custom fields and workflows, AI Essentials and 50 automation actions per seat a month. Business at $25 covers 5 to 200 users and adds the agency features: proofing and approvals on all asset types, dynamic request forms with branching logic, native time tracking, workload and resource management, baselines and critical path, blueprints and templates, portfolio management and 200 automation actions per seat a month.

Monday's Basic at $9 covers unlimited items and boards, unlimited free read-only viewers, Kanban view and 5 GB of storage — but no automations, no integrations and no guest access, which makes it a poor fit for most agencies. Standard at $12 adds Timeline, Gantt and Calendar views, guest access at 4 guests per billed seat, 250 automation and 250 integration actions a month, and 20 GB of storage. Pro at $19 adds time tracking, private boards and docs, the workload view, dependency columns, 25,000 automation and 25,000 integration actions a month, unlimited guests and 100 GB of storage. Both vendors offer a 14-day trial with no credit card — Monday's trial is the Pro plan, automatically applied on signup.

One pricing detail to flag rather than state: Monday's AI credits. Since May 2026, new accounts buy an AI credit bundle with seats — 1,000 credits a month on Basic, 2,000 on Standard, 3,000 on Pro. Some sources report the credits are offset by a discount, so there is no extra cost at list price; one source claims they add roughly $80 a month on top of every subscription.

The agency math: 5 seats and 10 seats

Agencies rarely buy one seat, so the honest comparison prices the same team at 5 and 10 seats, on annual billing — and then applies the seat rules, because both vendors sell seats in blocks that change the bill for odd-sized teams.

Monday is cheaper at both tiers: entry $9 against $10 a seat, working tier $19 against $25 — Pro is 24% cheaper per seat than Business, and Wrike Business costs about 32% more per seat than Monday Pro. For a 5-person agency that is $360 a year saved at the working tier, and $720 a year at 10 seats. But Standard, not Basic, is arguably Monday's real entry tier for an agency: Basic has no automations or integrations at all, which most agencies need on day one, so the honest entry comparison is Monday Standard at $60 a month against Wrike Team at $50 — where Wrike is actually $10 a month cheaper for 5 users,

The seat rules are where small agencies get surprised. Wrike sells seats in blocks — groups of 5 up to 30 seats, 10 from 30 to 100, 25 above 100 — so a 6-person agency pays for 10 seats: $100 a month on Team, $250 on Business. Monday sells paid seats with a 3-seat minimum and buckets of 3, 5, 10, 15 and 20, so a 4-person agency pays for 5 seats and a 1- or 2-person team still pays for 3 ($27 a month minimum on Basic, annual). Wrike's Team tier also caps at 15 users, forcing a jump to Business at $25 when the sixteenth person joins; Monday has no such tier cap below 40 seats. On monthly billing, where Wrike publishes no prices, Monday's 5-user bill is Basic $60, Standard $70 and Pro $120 a month. Proofing and approvals: Wrike's clearest win

If your agency's work ends with a client saying "approved" on a design, a video or a document, this section is the decision. Wrike ships native visual proofing on Business: mark up an asset, compare versions side by side, and run multi-level approvals, with an Adobe Creative Cloud extension for the designers who live in those apps. It is the one feature in this comparison that Monday has no native equivalent for — on Monday, approvals are built from status columns and automations, or bought as a third-party app.

That matters because proofing is not a nice-to-have for a creative agency; it is the bottleneck. A design that goes through email comments, a shared drive and a chat thread loses versions, and the version a client approved is the version the team cannot find. Wrike's proofing keeps the markup, the version and the approval on the same task, and the approval itself becomes part of the project record.

The counterpoint is that not every agency proofs visual work. A web-development agency, a content agency or a media-buying agency may never mark up an image, and for them Wrike's proofing is a feature they pay for and never open. Monday's approval workflow — a status column that moves from "in review" to "approved", with an automation that notifies the next person — is cruder but free, and it works for text, links and files that do not need pixel-level markup. The test is simple: if your team's approvals happen on visuals, Wrike Business earns its premium. If they happen on checklists, it does not. Client access: Monday's cheaper window into the work

Agencies do not just manage work; they show it to clients. Here Monday is clearly stronger, and the gap is measured in money. Monday gives unlimited free read-only viewers from Basic — a client can watch a board without a paid seat — and guest access from Standard at 4 guests per billed seat, unlimited on Pro. A 5-person agency on Monday Pro can invite every client contact as a guest at no extra cost.

Wrike charges for external users as paid seats. Only "collaborators" — limited-function guests — are free, and they are capped at 20 invites or 15% of the licence count, whichever is greater. A 10-seat agency gets 20 free collaborator invites; beyond that, each client user is a paid seat at $10 or $25 a month. For an agency with five clients who each want two people watching the work, that is ten paid seats on top of the team's own — $100 to $250 a month of client-viewing cost that Monday charges $0 for.

Neither tool offers a true white-label client portal on a standard plan — both are general work-management tools adapted for client work, not client-portal products. The practical difference is the cost of transparency: Monday makes it free to let a client watch, Wrike makes it a line item. If your agency's pitch includes "you will always see where your project stands", Monday's model supports that pitch and Wrike's taxes it. Resource management and time tracking

Resource management is Wrike's second clear win. From Business, Wrike gives workload charts, capacity planning, effort allocation and bookings, with advanced capacity planning and job roles on Pinnacle. That is the toolkit for an agency that needs to know, before it signs a client, whether the team actually has the hours — and to prove it with bill rates and billable hours on the Pinnacle tier. Monday's workload view arrives on Pro and is shallower; full portfolio and resource management is Enterprise-only, behind a quote.

Time tracking splits the other way on price. Wrike's native time tracking is on Business at $25 a seat. Monday's native time-tracking column is on Pro at $19 — cheaper, but it requires the top standard tier. One comparison source claims Monday has no native time tracking and needs Toggl or Harvest; that is wrong — the time-tracking column is a documented Pro feature. Both vendors' entry tiers lack it, so an agency that bills by the hour starts at the working tier on either tool.

Automations are the quiet tiebreaker. Wrike Team allows 50 automation actions per seat a month and Business 200 per seat — the cap scales with team size. Monday Standard allows 250 automation and 250 integration actions a month for the whole account, not per seat, while Pro allows 25,000 of each. For an automation-heavy agency, Monday Pro's 25,000-action cap is far more generous than Wrike's per-seat model; for a small team that automates lightly, Wrike's per-seat allowance is plenty.

Who should pick which — and the catches

Pick Wrike if your agency lives on client approvals of visual work — proofing and multi-level approvals on Business are the deciding feature; if resource planning and capacity matter enough to pay $25 a seat; if your team is 15 people or fewer and fits the Team tier, or 5 to 200 and needs the Business feature set; and if your clients do not need free guest seats, or you have fewer than 20 collaborators to invite.

Pick Monday if price decides it — Pro is 24% cheaper per seat than Business at the working tier; if client visibility is part of the pitch, with unlimited free viewers and generous guest access; if your agency automates heavily, since Pro's 25,000-action cap dwarfs Wrike's per-seat allowance; if you want monthly billing as an option; or if your team is growing past 15 people and does not want a forced tier jump.

The catches, stated plainly. On Wrike: no published monthly prices, seat blocks that make a 6-person team pay for 10, the 15-user cap on Team, and paid seats for client users beyond the collaborator allowance. On Monday: Basic has no automations or integrations, so the real entry tier is Standard; the AI-credit cost question is unresolved and could add to the bill; and full resource management is Enterprise-only. Prices were checked November 12, 2026 — Wrike's from its official pricing page, Monday's from multiple independent reads of monday.com/pricing in October 2026 — confirm both on the vendor pages before you buy.

Frequently asked questions

Which is cheaper for a small agency, Wrike or Monday?

Monday, at the working tier. Wrike Business costs $25 per user a month on annual billing; Monday Pro costs $19 — about 24% cheaper per seat. For 5 users that is $125 a month ($1,500 a year) on Wrike Business against $95 a month ($1,140 a year) on Monday Pro.

Does Monday have proofing and approvals like Wrike?

No native proofing. Wrike Business includes visual proofing with markup, version comparison and multi-level approvals, plus an Adobe Creative Cloud extension. Monday has no native proofing tool — approvals are built from status columns and automations, or added through a third-party app. If your agency's approvals happen on visuals, Wrike's proofing is its clearest advantage.

Does Wrike have a free plan?

Yes. Wrike Free costs $0 with unlimited users, 2 GB of storage per account, 200 active tasks, and board and table views. It has no automations. Monday's free plan is tighter: maximum 2 seats, 3 boards, 200 items total and 500 MB of storage, with no automations, integrations, guests or timeline views. Both offer a 14-day trial of a paid plan with no credit card required.

Which is better for client work, Wrike or Monday?

Monday, for client visibility: unlimited free read-only viewers from Basic and guest access from Standard (4 guests per billed seat, unlimited on Pro). Wrike charges for external users as paid seats; only limited-function collaborators are free, capped at 20 invites or 15% of licences. Wrike is better for the work itself when it needs proofing, resource planning and structured delivery. Neither offers a true white-label client portal on a standard plan.

Can I migrate from Wrike to Monday (or back)?

Both products export projects and tasks to CSV and offer import tooling, but neither vendor publishes a one-click migration for the other, and migration fidelity — boards, automations, proofing history, time logs, guest permissions — was not tested for this article. Budget a manual migration and a data check if you switch, and export your data before the old subscription ends.

Conclusion

Wrike vs Monday for agencies comes down to what the agency actually sells. If the work ends with a client approving a visual, and the agency needs to plan capacity before it signs the next client, Wrike Business at $25 a seat earns its premium — proofing and resource management are the two features Monday cannot match without add-ons or the Enterprise tier. If the work is managed in boards, shown to clients for free, and automated heavily, Monday Pro at $19 a seat is the better buy, and the $360 a year saved on a 5-person team is real money. The deciding test is a week on the trial of each: run one real client project through proofing on Wrike and one through guest access on Monday, and the right tool will be obvious by Friday.
