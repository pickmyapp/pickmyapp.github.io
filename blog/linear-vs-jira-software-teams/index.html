Linear vs Jira for Software Teams: Which Should You Pick? (2026)

  • Project Management
  • Published Oct 13, 2026
  • Updated: October 2026
  • 12 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

If your team is under about 15 developers and wants to ship fast, choose Linear: you can be running in an afternoon, paid plans start at $10 per seat per month billed annually, and it includes an AI agent even on the free tier. Choose Jira if you need custom workflows, granular permissions, compliance certifications, or non-engineering teams on the same tracker. Free tiers: Jira covers up to 10 users; Linear covers unlimited members but only 250 issues.

What is Linear?

Linear is a purpose-built issue tracker for software teams: fast, keyboard-driven, and deliberately opinionated about how product work should run. Founded in San Francisco in 2019, it organizes work into issues with a simple state machine (Backlog → To do → In Progress → Done), time-boxed cycles instead of sprints, projects for grouping issues, roadmaps for planning, and a triage inbox for incoming requests.

The philosophy is explicit: no custom issue types beyond a small set, no drag-and-drop workflow builder, no marketplace of thousands of apps. What you get instead is speed — the team benchmarks its UI at sub-100-millisecond response times — deep Git and Slack integrations, and a clean default process that needs no configuration project. On G2 it scores about 4.6 out of 5, notably higher than Jira's 4.3, albeit from far fewer reviews.

What is Jira?

Jira is Atlassian's configurable project platform: custom workflows, Scrum and Kanban boards, backlogs, sprints, story-point estimation, and reporting built on Jira Query Language (JQL). It is the default issue tracker at large organizations because it can model almost any process — custom issue types, custom fields, multi-step approval workflows, granular permissions — and because it plugs into the Atlassian ecosystem: Confluence for docs, Bitbucket for code, and Jira Service Management for IT support.

That configurability is also the reputation problem: the UI shows its age, setup takes days to weeks, and most organizations end up naming a Jira administrator. It is the safe enterprise pick — and overkill for a team that just needs to track what ships this week.

On paper, Jira undercuts Linear at the entry tier: roughly $7.91 per user per month for Standard versus $10 for Linear Basic. Two caveats change the math. First, Linear's paid plans require annual billing — you cannot go month to month — while Jira lets you pay monthly at a modest premium. Second, Jira's free tier is genuinely more useful for a real team: 10 users with no issue cap, versus Linear's free tier which caps you at 250 issues (unlimited members, but you will hit the issue ceiling fast).

The real cost: Jira's marketplace tax

Jira's headline price is rarely the invoice. The Atlassian Marketplace hosts thousands of apps — advanced time tracking, test management, diagramming, reporting — and most are billed per user per month on top of your Jira subscription. Two or three common apps at $2–$3 per seat each can add 50% to a Standard bill, and the apps that fill Jira's gaps (roadmaps beyond single-project planning, better docs, richer automation) are exactly the ones teams buy. Linear, by contrast, bundles its Git integrations, Slack integration, cycles, roadmaps, and docs into the seat price with no app marketplace to tempt you.

For a 15-person team: Linear Basic costs $150/month all-in on annual billing. Jira Standard lists around $119/month — but add time tracking and a reporting app at $2.50 per seat each and you are near $194/month. Teams wanting cross-project planning or a 99.9% SLA land on Premium at about $14.54 per seat, versus $16 for Linear Business. For most small teams, total cost is a wash — decide on workflow fit, not the sticker price. Our ClickUp pricing breakdown covers a third option.

How fast is setup, really?

This is Linear's biggest win and it is not close. Sign up, create a workspace, invite the team, and you are writing tickets in about five minutes. Cycles, projects, and roadmaps use sensible defaults, so there is nothing to configure before the first sprint, and almost nothing to administer afterward.

Jira asks you dozens of questions before you see your first board: project type, Scrum or Kanban, administrators, issue types, workflow states, permissions, notifications, automation rules. A small team typically spends hours to days on initial configuration, and the instance keeps demanding attention — permission cleanups, workflow adjustments, notification tuning. Whichever tool you pick, name the person who owns the setup before rollout — that decision predicts the outcome better than the tool does.

Quick fact: Teams migrating off Jira report that complex workflows and permission schemes have no equivalent in Linear — which is usually the point of the move.

Opinionated vs configurable: the core tradeoff

Linear's opinionated model

Linear gives you one workflow per team with a fixed set of states, a small set of issue types, and limited custom fields. Cycles are your sprints; triage is your intake. You cannot build a five-step approval chain with conditional transitions, and you cannot create a custom issue type for every flavor of work. For a product team, this is liberating: the tool removes process debates instead of hosting them. For a team with a genuinely unusual process, it is a wall.

Jira's configurable model

Jira gives you a no-code workflow editor where you can define any set of statuses, transitions, conditions, and validators. Custom issue types and unlimited custom fields let you track hardware, compliance artifacts, or content pipelines alongside software. Granular permissions control who can see, edit, or transition each item. Automation rules ship on Standard and scale up on Premium. The price of this power is governance: without an owner, Jira instances accumulate workflows, fields, and schemes until nobody understands the system — the classic "Jira admin tax."

When configurability wins

Choose configurability when the process must be exact and audited — regulated industries, multi-department handoffs, compliance controls. Choose opinionated when the team is small enough that process lives in people's heads, which describes most teams under 50.

Integrations and ecosystem

For day-to-day developer workflows, both cover the essentials: GitHub and GitLab integrations that link commits and branches to issues, Slack integrations that create issues from messages, and full REST and GraphQL APIs. Linear auto-closes issues from commits; Jira's dev-tool integrations run deeper if you are already on Bitbucket.

The ecosystem gap is real but one-sided. Jira's marketplace offers thousands of third-party apps — test management, portfolio planning, advanced reporting — which matters when you need something Atlassian never built. Linear has no app marketplace; it offers a focused set of native integrations instead. The practical consequence: Jira can grow into anything, at the cost of per-seat app fees and integration sprawl. Linear stays lean, at the cost of "sorry, that app doesn't exist."

Docs are the sharpest ecosystem difference: Jira pairs with Confluence, a separate product and a separate bill, while Linear includes docs on paid plans — one less subscription for a startup.

How do the AI features compare?

Linear puts AI closest to the daily workflow. The Linear Agent, currently in beta, is available even on the free tier and can draft issues, break down work, and answer questions about your backlog. On Business plans, Triage Intelligence auto-routes incoming issues to the right team.

Jira's AI layer is Rovo, included with Standard, Premium, and Enterprise plans since Atlassian rolled it out across tiers in 2025. Rovo Chat answers natural-language questions about project state, Rovo agents run repeatable checks like flagging work items not ready for a sprint, and Rovo Dev turns work items into code changes, and admins can disable AI entirely. For in-tracker AI on a small team, Linear leads; for AI spanning Confluence, Bitbucket, and Service Management, Atlassian has the broader surface. If your team is weighing general-purpose PM tools too, see how Notion and Asana compare.

What does migrating involve?

Moving from Jira to Linear is the well-trodden path: Linear ships a Jira importer that brings over issues, comments, and attachments, and simple setups transfer in an afternoon. Complex workflows get simplified to Linear's state machine, permission schemes get dropped, and custom fields get trimmed — which most teams treat as a feature, since the migration forces a long-postponed process cleanup.

Migrating from Linear to Jira is harder, because you are moving from simplicity into a system that expects configuration: you rebuild workflows, permissions, and schemes that Linear never had. The asymmetry matters for planning: starting on Linear and outgrowing it is a cheaper mistake than starting on Jira and finding it too heavy, because the Linear-to-Jira move happens once you know exactly what process you need.

Which should you choose: Linear or Jira?

Choose Linear if

  • Your team is mostly engineers and product people who want to ship, not configure.
  • You want to be running in an afternoon with near-zero administration.
  • Your process fits issues, cycles, and roadmaps without custom approval chains.
  • You want predictable per-seat pricing with no marketplace app tax.
  • You like having an AI agent in the tracker from day one, even on the free tier.

Choose Jira if

  • Your process must be modeled exactly — custom workflows, approvals, granular permissions.
  • You face compliance requirements (ISO 27001, HIPAA, FedRAMP) or audited controls.
  • Multiple departments (support, HR, marketing) share one tracker.
  • You are already invested in the Atlassian ecosystem (Confluence, Bitbucket).
  • You need a marketplace app for something neither tool builds natively.

Frequently asked questions

Is Linear cheaper than Jira?

At list price, Jira's Standard plan (around $7.91 per user per month) undercuts Linear's Basic plan ($10 per user per month, billed annually). But the gap closes fast: Jira marketplace apps are billed per seat, and teams that need premium Jira features land on Premium at about $14.54 per user per month, versus $16 for Linear Business. For most small teams, total cost is a wash — decide on workflow fit instead.

Can Linear replace Jira for a large company?

For a single product or engineering organization, yes — teams of several hundred run on Linear. It cannot replace Jira where the organization needs granular permissions, audited custom workflows, compliance certifications such as ISO 27001, HIPAA, or FedRAMP, or multiple departments sharing one tracker. Those are structural gaps, not missing features.

Is Jira too heavy for a startup?

Usually, yes. Jira asks you to model your workflow before you have written a single ticket: project type, issue types, workflow states, permissions, and notifications. A small team spends hours to days configuring before getting value, and someone has to keep administering it. Startups that just need issues, cycles, and a roadmap get to value in an afternoon with Linear.

Can I migrate from Jira to Linear?

Yes. Linear ships a Jira importer that brings over issues, comments, and attachments. Simple setups transfer cleanly. Complex Jira workflows, permission schemes, and custom fields have no equivalent in Linear and must be simplified or dropped — which is usually the point. Migrating back from Linear to Jira is harder, because you rebuild the configuration Jira expects.

Which has better AI features in 2026: Linear or Jira?

Linear puts AI closer to the daily workflow: the Linear Agent (in beta) is available even on the free tier, and Triage Intelligence on Business plans auto-routes incoming issues. Jira's Rovo AI is included with Standard, Premium, and Enterprise plans and covers chat-based project questions, automated checks, and code generation via Rovo Dev. For in-tracker AI, Linear leads; for org-wide AI across the Atlassian suite, Jira has the edge.

Conclusion

For a startup or small software team, Linear is the better default: an afternoon of setup, a fast opinionated workflow, predictable pricing, and AI in the tracker from day one. Jira earns its complexity when the organization demands it — regulated industries, audited processes, multiple departments on one system, or a deep Atlassian investment. Start with the tool that matches the team you have; you can always graduate to Jira when the process outgrows Linear's opinions.
