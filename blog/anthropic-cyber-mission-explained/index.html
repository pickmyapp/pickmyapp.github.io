On October 8, 2026, Anthropic announced something unusual for an AI lab: not a model, but a defensive cybersecurity program. The Anthropic Cyber Mission aims its AI at defenders — open-source maintainers and critical-infrastructure operators — to find and fix vulnerabilities before attackers do. Its centerpiece is OSS Scanner, a free, opt-in service that scans eligible open-source projects with Anthropic's strongest models, including Claude Mythos.

This guide covers what was actually announced, how the scanner works, how it differs from existing scanners, and what to do whether you are a maintainer or a small business whose products depend on open-source code. Facts are framed as reported: the program is days old and several details are not yet published.

What is the Anthropic Cyber Mission?

The Anthropic Cyber Mission is a long-term initiative from Anthropic to direct its AI models toward defensive cybersecurity: finding and fixing vulnerabilities in open-source software and critical infrastructure. It launched with two programs.

The first is the Critical Infrastructure Defense Program. As reported, Anthropic is putting frontier Claude models, engineers, and threat research with the security firms and integrators that already serve operators of power grids, water systems, transportation, factories, and government systems — operational technology that often cannot be taken offline to patch. Eleven founding partners were named, including CrowdStrike, Palo Alto Networks, Deloitte, Accenture, and Dragos.

The second is OSS Scanner, the free vulnerability scanner this guide covers. Anthropic says it was inspired by Google's OSS-Fuzz and informed by its own Project Glasswing, an internal AI vulnerability-research effort. As context, over six months Anthropic's models surfaced more than 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities, of which humans reviewed roughly 6,000 and 584 advisories were issued.

Quick fact: Early coverage reported more than 100 enrollment requests on the scanner's public repository within days of launch — demand was immediate.

OSS Scanner, explained

OSS Scanner is a free, opt-in service that runs periodic security scans of eligible open-source projects with Anthropic's strongest models and emails maintainers machine-written vulnerability reports. No fee, no trial, no upsell in the announcement. Here is the pipeline, as described in the program's public repository and coverage of it.

How the scanning pipeline works

A core maintainer submits a pull request to Anthropic's public oss-scanner GitHub repository, and Anthropic manually confirms the applicant's maintainer status. The pipeline builds the project inside an isolated virtual machine (internet enabled at this stage so the build can fetch dependencies), then moves the environment offline for scanning. Inside the sandbox, agents hunt for bugs while others double-check findings, propose patches, and trace root causes; results are emailed to the project's primary contact, with an optional GPG key encrypting reports.

What every report includes

Each report is self-contained: an explanation of the flaw, a proof-of-concept reproducer demonstrating it is exploitable, plus where possible the commit that introduced it and a candidate fix. Findings can be attributed as "Discovered by Anthropic's OSS Scanner" with an identifier such as ANT-2026-ABCD1234.

What has not been published yet

Anthropic calls the scans "periodic" but has not published a schedule — whether projects are scanned weekly, monthly, or otherwise is not yet announced. Supported languages, per-project scan quotas, and any delivery options beyond email are likewise not yet published. Treat third-party claims on those points as speculation.

Key terms, explained simply

Cybersecurity coverage assumes background knowledge. Here are the plain-English definitions behind this story.

Vulnerability scanning

Vulnerability scanning is the automated analysis of software to find security weaknesses an attacker could exploit. Older scanners match known bad patterns or check dependency flaw lists; OSS Scanner works like a security auditor, reasoning about logic errors, memory bugs, and unsafe input handling.

Open-source supply chain

The open-source supply chain is the chain of shared libraries and packages your software quietly depends on. A typical web app is built from hundreds of them. When one has a flaw — the Log4Shell bug in Log4j is the famous example — every product including it becomes vulnerable at once. OSS Scanner targets this layer.

SBOM (software bill of materials)

An SBOM is an inventory of every software component inside a product — an ingredient list for code. It records which packages are included and at which versions, so when a new flaw is disclosed you can answer the only question that matters: are we affected?

Fuzzing and OSS-Fuzz

Fuzzing is throwing huge volumes of random or malformed inputs at software to find crashes. Google's OSS-Fuzz has run this as a free service since 2016 and is the cited inspiration for OSS Scanner. The method differs: fuzzing crashes code to find bugs, AI auditing reads code. They catch different flaws, so maintainers may want both.

Proof of concept and coordinated disclosure

A proof of concept is a working demonstration that a vulnerability is real — the "show me" behind every report. Coordinated disclosure gives maintainers a private window, commonly 90 days, to fix a validated flaw before it goes public. Anthropic will not apply a 90-day clock to unvalidated, model-generated findings — the clock starts only when a human confirms a finding.

How it differs from existing scanners

Free scanning already exists, so the honest question is what OSS Scanner adds: existing tools are something you run on your code, while OSS Scanner is something a frontier AI lab runs for open-source projects — with reasoning ability that catches the logic flaws pattern scanners miss.

Quick fact: The CyberGym benchmark, cited by Anthropic, reportedly showed AI detection rates rising from under 20% to over 85% in about a year — the jump that makes OSS Scanner plausible now.

The boldest decision: no human review

Every OSS Scanner report is generated and delivered without any human reading it first. Anthropic is explicit: removing people from the loop is what lets scans run faster and more often, and as reported, some maintainers had already asked for bulk unreviewed findings rather than waiting for triaged batches.

The upside is scale. In Project Glasswing, Anthropic reported surfacing more than 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities in six months — far beyond what any human team could triage. Maintainers described useful results: as reported, curl's Daniel Stenberg flagged multiple real issues including one of the worst curl vulnerabilities in years, and wolfSSL's Todd Ouska said all but two of 74 reports were valid, with five becoming CVEs.

The downside is noise: Anthropic expects a true-positive rate above 90%, with early reported checks at about 88% and 97% on small samples, while conceding reports may be wrong or mis-severitied. For maintainers, a confident but wrong report is a new kind of toil, and validation is the human work Anthropic says has not gotten cheaper. Its honest framing: attackers keep a near-term advantage because finding flaws got cheap while fixing stays human; it forecasts AI will favor defense within two years.

What it means for open-source maintainers

If you maintain an open-source project, OSS Scanner is a free security team you did not hire — one that never sleeps, occasionally hallucinates, and emails you directly.

Who is eligible

Eligibility is narrow and decided case by case, following the OSS-Fuzz playbook: projects with critical impact on infrastructure and user security, especially libraries exposed to remote attack — code parsing untrusted network input is the classic example — with many users and dependents. The program suits teams that already handle verified high- and critical-severity reports; widely used networking, cryptography, or parsing libraries are the target, while a weekend utility library is unlikely to qualify.

How to enroll

A core maintainer opens a pull request on Anthropic's public oss-scanner GitHub repository, adding a configuration file with the repository URL, a contact email, and a Dockerfile that builds the project for the offline agents. Optional: extra contacts, a threat-model file, and a GPG key for encrypted reports. Anthropic manually verifies core-maintainer status and retains discretion to approve or withdraw projects. Not yet published: how many projects will be accepted and how long review takes.

What to expect after enrollment

Even at the hoped-for 90%-plus true-positive rate, one in ten reports is noise. If your project is already drowning in issues, adding an automated firehose is a deliberate decision — and a configuration switch can pause reports if needed.

What it means for small businesses

You cannot enroll your company — OSS Scanner scans open-source projects, not businesses. But your website, app, or tools almost certainly run on open-source software, and this changes your risk picture: the shared libraries underneath your business get more AI scrutiny at no cost to maintainers, so dependency bugs should surface earlier. The uncomfortable side is that Anthropic says finding flaws is getting cheaper while fixing them stays human work — so the window between a flaw being discoverable and your systems being patched is what matters, and your patching discipline closes it.

Notice the direction of travel: AI is moving from chatbots into defensive infrastructure, from AI guardians in security suites to frontier-model explainers like our Gemini 4 Argon guide and Claude in Google Workspace breakdown. What your business should do this month

OSS Scanner is a reason to act, not a product to buy:

If you ship software

  • Build an SBOM: list every open-source dependency and version, regenerated on every release.
  • Subscribe to security advisories for your dependencies — Dependabot, your stack's feeds, your framework's security list.
  • Automate dependency updates so fixes ship as fast as scanners find flaws.
  • Maintain a widely used library yourself? Evaluate an OSS Scanner enrollment request.

If you run a website or SaaS

  • Keep your platform current: CMS, plugins, themes, server packages. Most small-business breaches come from unpatched known flaws.
  • Ask vendors whether they publish an SBOM and how they handle dependency disclosures.
  • Review your hosting stack — our cheap WordPress hosting guide covers what to look for in update-friendly hosts.

The theme in both columns: the scanner makes the ecosystem's bugs visible faster, which makes your response time the deciding factor. That takes a checklist and a calendar reminder, not a security team.

Open questions and what to watch

The program is days old, so several answers are honestly "not yet published": scan cadence, supported languages, and how many projects will be accepted. The number to watch is the production true-positive rate — that decides whether OSS Scanner becomes essential infrastructure or an experiment. Watch too whether other frontier labs follow with rival free scanners.

Frequently asked questions

Is Anthropic OSS Scanner really free?

Yes, as announced: OSS Scanner runs periodic scans of eligible open-source projects at no cost. There is no published fee or paid tier. The catches: Anthropic decides eligibility case by case, and reports arrive unreviewed, so your team validates them.

How do I get my open-source project scanned by Anthropic?

A core maintainer opens a pull request on Anthropic's public oss-scanner GitHub repository, adding a configuration file with the repository URL, a contact email, and a Dockerfile that builds the project. Optional: a threat-model file and a GPG key for encrypted reports. Anthropic manually verifies core-maintainer status, and eligibility is judged case by case.

Why doesn't Anthropic review the reports before sending them?

Anthropic says skipping human review lets scans run faster and more often — in Project Glasswing, models surfaced over 29,000 candidate vulnerabilities in six months, beyond what any human team could triage. The trade-off: maintainers validate every report and should expect false positives.

How accurate is the OSS Scanner?

Anthropic expects a true-positive rate above 90%. Early reported checks are encouraging but small: 85 of 97 findings validated (about 88%), and wolfSSL reported 72 of 74 valid (about 97%). Treat these as early, company-reported figures — the production rate is not yet published.

I'm not a maintainer. What should my small business do about this?

You cannot enroll, but your software supply chain is both the beneficiary and the exposure. Build an SBOM of every dependency and version, subscribe to security advisories, automate updates, and keep platforms patched. Faster discovery only helps if your response time keeps up.

Conclusion

The Anthropic Cyber Mission is the clearest signal yet that frontier AI labs see defensive cybersecurity as part of their job. OSS Scanner's offer — free, recurring, AI-reasoned scans with a working exploit attached — is genuinely new, and early maintainer reports suggest the signal is real. The gamble is the no-human-review design: speed and scale at the price of noise. For critical-reach projects, enrollment is worth evaluating. For everyone else: know your dependencies, watch the advisories, and patch quickly — the scanners are getting faster, and your response time has to match.
