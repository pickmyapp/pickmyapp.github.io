Squarespace vs Format for photographers (2026)

  • Website Builders
  • Published Oct 15, 2026
  • Updated: October 2026
  • 10 min read
  • By PickMyApp Research Team
Quick answer

For most working photographers, Format is the better pick: it is built only for photographers, costs less (plans reported around $10 to $26 per month on annual billing), and includes client galleries, favorites-based proofing, watermarking, and Format Store print sales as native features. Choose Squarespace (from $16 per month annual) instead if you want the most polished portfolio design, a serious blog, strong SEO, and a full-featured online store for selling much more than prints. If client proofing is central to your business, Format wins; if storytelling and marketing are, Squarespace wins.

A photographer's website has one job that matters above everything else: make your images look expensive. Squarespace and Format both promise exactly that, but they arrive at it from opposite directions. Squarespace is a general website builder that happens to make very beautiful portfolio sites. Format is a portfolio builder that only exists for photographers and other visual creatives. That difference in DNA shows up everywhere — in the pricing, in how clients review their photos, and in how you sell your work. This comparison looks at both from a working photographer's perspective: cost, portfolio design, client galleries and proofing, watermarking, print sales, blogging, and SEO. If you are still weighing the wider field, start with our roundup of the best website builders for photographers for the full landscape.

Squarespace: the all-round builder

Squarespace is a general-purpose website builder known for elegant, design-led templates, and its portfolio galleries are among the prettiest on any mainstream platform. You get unlimited pages, galleries, and bandwidth on every plan, plus a free custom domain for the first year on annual billing. Every template is mobile-responsive, images are served with automatic scaling, and galleries come in several layouts — grids, slideshows, full-bleed strips — that look refined without much effort.

The catch for photographers is that Squarespace treats your portfolio the same way it treats a restaurant menu or an architect's case studies. The galleries are public display spaces. There is no native way for a client to log in, favorite their picks, leave comments on individual images, or download files with your watermark applied. Squarespace also bundles real extras photographers care about: a genuinely good blogging platform, built-in SEO controls, email campaigns, and a full online store. If your photography business leans heavily on content marketing — publishing shoots, writing location guides, ranking in Google — Squarespace gives you the strongest all-in-one foundation.

Format: built only for photographers

Format exists for one audience: photographers, artists, and designers who need a portfolio website with professional client workflows. Every theme in its library is designed around visual work, and the feature list reads like a photographer's business checklist: private client galleries, favorites and proofing, watermarks, print sales through Format Store, large file transfer, and unlimited bandwidth on all paid plans. It even offers Workflow add-ons for contracts and project management, although those are a separate purchase unless you are on a higher-tier plan.

Format is deliberately not a general website builder. Its blogging tool is basic, its e-commerce is photography-oriented rather than general-purpose, and the total number of themes is smaller than what the big platforms offer. What you get in return is a site where the client workflow is first-class: a wedding photographer can shoot on Saturday, upload a private gallery on Monday, have the couple favorite their album picks on Tuesday, and take print orders through the built-in store — all under one subscription, with no third-party gallery service required.

How does Squarespace pricing compare with Format pricing?

Squarespace charges more, but it also sells you more. Format is the cheaper subscription across the board, and its mid-tier plans already include the photographer-specific features that would cost extra as add-ons elsewhere. What the headline price leaves out

On Squarespace, a wedding or portrait photographer who needs proofing and delivery has to add a gallery service to the stack. Pixieset, ShootProof, and Pic-Time — the usual choices — typically cost $8 to $24 per month on their own entry and mid plans. A Squarespace Business site ($23/mo annual) plus a mid-tier gallery service can easily reach $40 to $47 a month. Format's Pro or Pro Plus plan (~$16 to $26/mo annual) already includes those client workflows natively, so the total bill is usually lower.

Running the other direction, selling prints on Squarespace's Business plan costs you a 3% transaction fee on every sale (on top of Stripe or PayPal processing), which makes the jump to Commerce Basic ($27/mo annual, zero Squarespace transaction fees) worth doing once print revenue becomes meaningful. If you plan to sell merchandise, presets, or workshops alongside prints, Squarespace's store features justify the higher subscription, a trade-off we cover in our Squarespace vs Shopify comparison for selling online.

Quick fact: Format includes unlimited bandwidth on all paid plans, so a viral image or a busy gallery link never costs you extra. Squarespace also offers unlimited bandwidth and storage on all plans.

Which has better portfolio design and image presentation?

Template quality and choice

Squarespace's portfolio templates are the benchmark for the whole industry — typography, spacing, and full-bleed image treatments that look editorial straight out of the box. Format's theme library is smaller but every theme is built for visual portfolios, and higher-tier plans unlock premium design elements and deeper customization. If you want your site to look like a magazine feature with minimal effort, Squarespace has the edge. If you want themes tuned specifically for how photographers show series, projects, and galleries, Format is at least its equal.

Image handling

Both platforms handle the photography fundamentals: responsive images, automatic resizing, and fast global delivery. Squarespace automatically serves scaled image variants and supports high-resolution display. Format is built around unlimited bandwidth and large file handling, which matters when clients are reviewing full-resolution galleries. Neither platform applies aggressive compression that would embarrass your work, but as always, optimize your exports before uploading — no builder rescues a 30 MB homepage hero.

Client galleries, proofing, and watermarking

This is the section where Format pulls decisively ahead, because this workflow is the core of its product. Format's client galleries let you share branded, password-protected galleries where clients can view, favorite, and download images. Watermarking protects your proofs, and downloads can be controlled per gallery. Older coverage of Format describes proofing-style favorites workflows as a headline feature, and it remains the reason many wedding and portrait photographers choose the platform.

Squarespace has no equivalent. Its galleries are public display tools; clients cannot favorite, comment on, or select images, and there is no native watermarking or per-gallery download control. Squarespace photographers typically handle proofing and delivery in a separate service — Pixieset and ShootProof are the common picks — which works fine but means two subscriptions, two logins, and a client experience split across two brands. For a beginner who just needs a portfolio, this does not matter. For a working photographer delivering hundreds of client galleries a year, it is the single biggest reason to prefer Format. Which is better for selling prints and products?

Format Store

Format Store lets you sell prints and digital products directly from your site or galleries, which fits the photography business model neatly: a client favorites their wedding images and orders prints from the same gallery. The store is photography-oriented rather than general-purpose, and higher-tier plans raise the product limit (older plan breakdowns put the entry store at just a few products, scaling up significantly on the top tiers). If prints and digital downloads are your entire catalog, it is exactly the right amount of store.

Squarespace Commerce

Squarespace's store is a genuine general e-commerce platform: product variants, inventory, discount codes, abandoned cart recovery, shipping integrations, POS, and — on Commerce Advanced — customer accounts and commerce APIs. That is overkill if you only sell a dozen print sizes, but it matters if your business also sells workshops, presets, gift cards, or merchandise. The trade-off is cost: you are paying Commerce-tier prices and, on Business, a 3% transaction fee, for selling power Format never tries to match.

Blogging and SEO

Squarespace is clearly the better platform if your marketing runs on content. Its blogging tools support scheduled posts, categories, tags, author profiles, and even podcast hosting, and its SEO controls — editable titles, descriptions, URLs, automatic XML sitemaps, and solid mobile performance — are among the best of any hosted builder. Photographers who publish shoot recaps, location guides, or pricing explainers to rank in Google will get far more from Squarespace.

Format covers the SEO basics — custom domain, editable page metadata, mobile-responsive themes — but its blogging is rudimentary and it is not a content-marketing platform. If your clients find you through Instagram, referrals, and wedding directories rather than Google, Format's lighter blogging will not hold you back. If organic search is your growth channel, it will.

Which is easier to use?

Both are genuinely beginner-friendly, and both offer 14-day free trials with no credit card games. Squarespace's editor is more powerful and correspondingly a little more to learn; its design controls give you more ways to make the site yours, which also means more ways to fiddle instead of publishing. Format's editor is simpler and more opinionated — pick a photography theme, swap in your images, set up galleries — which gets a portfolio live faster. Support is 24/7 on both platforms (live chat and email; Squarespace's phone support has limited hours). For a broader take on how Squarespace compares with the other mainstream builder photographers consider, see our Wix vs Squarespace comparison.

The verdict: which photographer are you?

Choose Format if

  • You shoot weddings, portraits, families, or events and deliver client galleries regularly.
  • You want proofing, watermarking, and print sales in one subscription with no third-party gallery service.
  • You prefer the lowest total monthly bill for a working photography business.
  • You find clients through social media, referrals, and directories rather than blogging.

Choose Squarespace if

  • You want the most polished portfolio design with the least effort.
  • Blogging and SEO are central to how you get clients.
  • You sell more than prints — workshops, presets, merchandise — and want a full store.
  • You want one platform for a brand site plus marketing tools like email campaigns.

If you are just starting out and need a beautiful portfolio on a budget with zero client workflows, either works — but Format's lower price and photography-first themes make it the easier recommendation. If you already run a gallery service you love and just need the site, Squarespace's design and blogging are worth the premium.

Frequently asked questions

Is Format better than Squarespace for photographers?

For client-facing work, usually yes. Format is built only for photographers, so client galleries, favorites-based proofing, watermarking, and Format Store print sales are native features. Squarespace has more polished general design and stronger blogging and e-commerce, but no native proofing workflow, so you would need a separate gallery service like Pixieset.

Can photographers sell prints on Squarespace?

Yes, through its regular online store. On the Business plan you pay a 3% transaction fee on top of payment processing; the Commerce Basic and Commerce Advanced plans remove that fee. Format's Store is simpler and photography-oriented, but Squarespace's store is more powerful for selling many products with variants, discounts, and shipping rules.

Does Squarespace have client proofing galleries?

No, not natively. Squarespace galleries are designed for public portfolio display, not for clients to favorite, comment, and select images. Most photographers on Squarespace pair it with a dedicated gallery tool such as Pixieset, ShootProof, or Pic-Time for proofing and delivery.

How much does Format cost compared to Squarespace?

Format's portfolio plans are reported at roughly $10 to $26 per month on annual billing, which undercuts Squarespace's $16 to $49 per month. Format also includes client galleries, proofing, and unlimited bandwidth on all paid plans, while comparable client-workflow features on Squarespace require paid third-party add-ons.

Which is better for SEO, Squarespace or Format?

Squarespace is the stronger SEO platform: it includes clean meta controls, automatic sitemaps, blogging with scheduling, and solid mobile performance out of the box. Format covers SEO basics such as custom domains and metadata, but it is not a blogging platform, so photographers who rely on content marketing usually do better on Squarespace.

Conclusion

Squarespace and Format answer different versions of the same question. Squarespace asks, "What is the most beautiful website you can build?" Format asks, "What does a working photographer actually need?" For wedding, portrait, and event photographers, Format's native client galleries, proofing, watermarking, and lower price make it the more practical business tool. For editorial, fine-art, and blogger photographers — and anyone whose marketing runs on content — Squarespace's design polish, blogging, and SEO justify the premium. Trial both for their 14 free days with your real images before you commit; the right answer becomes obvious once your own work is on the screen.
